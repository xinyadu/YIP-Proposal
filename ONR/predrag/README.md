The rewards can be gamed, and the group's decisions are imperfect labels; each opens with a bold sentence. The third challenge, saying whose messages helped, is cut as not a selling point. ## RG3 edits (Oct 28): response to Vibhav's comment

Vibhav asked what keeps wrong group decisions from reinforcing bad behavior in RG3 ([vibhav_feedback.md](vibhav_feedback.md)). Targeted edits to `main-full.tex`, adding about ten lines; the overflow past page 20 is left for the final compression pass:

- **Positioning (RG3 challenges):** where a program can check the answer (math, code), RLVR trains on the answer key (Lambert et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2025, two new references); RG3 extends such training to questions without one (vessel V, a paper's claims), on which tools can check at most some steps.
- **Which decisions are labels (RG3 overview, Task 3):** first answers are weighted by how confident the rest of the group is; debate messages are credited only where the judge settled the disputed step by re-running tool evidence. Such a decision is a "high-confidence label, not an answer key" (the tool's data can be out of date, and re-running settles only the disputed step). A decision settled by argument alone is not used as a label.
- **Known answers test the premise (RG3 evaluation, G5):** the benchmarks' known answers, which the rewards never use, measure how often the labels are wrong by topic (checking η < 1/2) and give an upper bound, training on the known answers (standard RLVR); G5 now asks for at least half of that gain.
- **Shared misconceptions (RG3 evaluation, Limitations):** Task 2.2's random human audits also estimate each topic's error rate, and training on a topic stops once an anytime-valid test puts that rate above a preset threshold below one half; the Limitations add that training would reinforce a misconception every agent shares until the audits reveal it. R4 now reads "Audits catch collusion and measure error rates but never settle a debate" (they already estimated ε_cal).
- **Unchanged:** the noisy-label identity, the chance-agreement term of eq. (5), dropping decisions that involve flagged agents, and keeping planted cases out of training. No related work added beyond the two RLVR references; Kenton et al. stays in the RG3 motivation.

## What changed in v4

- Format follows the NOFO proposal rules: 12-point Times, 1-inch margins, single spacing, four pages before the references. (v3 used 11-point type and 0.75-inch top and bottom margins.)
- Citations are blue author-year, e.g. (Li et al., 2024a).
- Each task has one core method, following the structure of the PI's NSF proposals and the Joshi and Chen ONR YIPs. Extra components from v3 move to the full proposal as sub-directions.
- **Three research goals of equal weight, organized around debate** (as in the PI's NSF proposals, each RG carries its own contribution). Each agent answers on its own. RG1 weighs each answer by its agent's track record, counting shared mistakes once, and picks debaters and judges so that no agent can favor itself. RG2 settles disputes by debate instead of open discussion: two representatives debate before an impartial judge, whose choice is the group's decision, so the agents need not agree. RG3 lets the agents improve themselves: reinforcement learning on the group's decisions credits each message for helping reach the decision rather than for winning, needs no human labels, and keeps working after the agents outgrow human judges. Figure 1 draws the three goals alike.
- **Prior work is "discussion"; ours is "debate".** Discussion: agents read one another's answers and revise until they agree, with no judge (what most multi-agent systems do). Debate: two representatives argue opposite answers before an impartial judge, whose choice decides. The paper never calls prior LLM discussion "multi-agent debate" (although Du et al. do), and calls ours "agentic debate" in the overview and RG2. Section 3: "Prior work on LLM discussion (Du et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a) is limited: majority voting explains most of its gain (Choi et al., 2025), and it assumes honest agents that exchange only text. Standard debate helps weaker judges (Irving et al., 2018; Khan et al., 2024), but trusts the judge, assumes the sides truly compete, and grounds claims at most in quoted text." These limits motivate the agentic harness: tools whose evidence the judge re-runs, and a control loop. Task 2.1 contrasts our control loop with "most LLM discussions [that] run fixed rounds of text between given models". The newest AI-judge papers (Hossain et al. 2026; Zhu et al. 2026) are no longer cited.
- **Task 2.1's main new ideas are the debate harness and tool use within it, in place of open discussion.** A harness is the software that turns models into agents: a sandbox with tools, a control loop, memory, and verification of results (Ning et al. 2026). Our control loop sets who speaks, who judges, and when to stop; most LLM debates run fixed rounds of text between given models. Tools are an optional add-on: debaters may use them, and the judge re-runs any tool evidence before it counts. Where no tool applies, the judge rules on argument alone, as in standard debate.
- **Audits only catch collusion, and there are two kinds** (Task 2.2). Automatic audits mix in planted cases, questions whose answers we know. Human audits have a person or a direct measurement find the right answer for a few real decisions chosen at random. Agents that audits flag lose weight in RG1. Nothing triggers a human audit, so its cost stays fixed.
- **RG3 is about self-improvement:** "Let agents self-improve with reinforcement learning on the group's decisions". Working beyond human judges is one benefit, not the goal. Section 3 now motivates RG3: "Nor can agents yet keep improving at scale without an answer key: rewarding majority agreement collapsed accuracy (Shafayat et al., 2025), rewarding debate wins taught agents to invent quotes (Kenton et al., 2026), and rewarding approval taught them to mislead people (Wen et al., 2025)." The RG3 intro is now one line, like RG1's and RG2's: "Self-improvement needs rewards that track correct answers, not votes or debate wins." Task 3 trains the agents on the group's final decisions, which need no human labels and are harder to game because shared mistakes count once and the judge re-runs tool evidence. Each message is credited for moving the group toward the decision; decisions backed by re-run tool evidence weigh more, decisions involving agents flagged by audits are dropped, and planted cases stay out of training so agents cannot learn to spot them. The main experiment compares this reward with rewards for majority agreement and for debate wins.
- **Evaluation baselines are kept simple:** a single AI judge, majority vote, and averaging with equal weights. Comparisons with the newest debate variants (tool-using debate, collaborative disagreement resolution) and their references were dropped.
- **Limitations are a short last paragraph of Section 4** (argument alone where no tool applies; Navy data and expert validation). Impact is now Section 5 and adds that, because people audit only a few decisions and training needs no human labels, the methods are built to scale to superhuman AI, whose answers and debates people cannot easily evaluate, helping keep it honest and safe.
- **"Why it will succeed"** says most tasks build on the PI's own previous work, and describes HiPRAG as our method for agentic search that grounds answers in retrieved evidence, cutting unnecessary searches to 2.3% while missing fewer needed ones.
- Removed: "verdict", "verified outcomes", the "flagged as unverified" label, and the separate "automatic tests" layer. The words "crux", "check", and "test" (other than benchmarks) are not used.
- **Section 1 frames the problem around LLM judges and gives the project a broad scope.** It opens the way the PI's NSF proposals and the Joshi and Chen YIPs do: context first (AI agents built on LLMs judge one another's answers, peer-review papers, supply the rewards that train new models (Lee et al. 2024, RLAIF), and vote or discuss), then "Yet": a single LLM judge has known biases, such as favoring longer answers or answers like its own (Zheng et al. 2023; Panickssery et al. 2024); collective reasoning can do better than a single judge (Verga et al. 2024; Du et al. 2024), but averaging or voting stays biased when the judges share mistakes. Then the bold central problem and the vessel example. The last paragraph: verifying each claim is slow; "We propose debate as a foundation for trustworthy evaluation by AI"; "To work among many AI agents and let them learn from it, debate must overcome the example's three failures" (shared mistakes, dishonest agents, a judge that sees only words). The failures are obstacles, not the project's scope.
- **RG1 is written for a general reader** ("Decide what to debate, and pick debaters and judges impartially"). Its opening line names the two problems it solves: a plain vote lets shared mistakes pile up, and agents choosing debaters can favor themselves. Task 1.1 weighs each answer by its agent's track record; with no clear leader, the question goes to debate. Task 1.2 then picks one representative per answer and a judge, and its peer ratings pick only who argues; the judge picks the answer. Task 1.1's track records also weight the raters in Task 1.2. PRD is now cited in Task 1.2, where peer ratings are used.
- Sections 2 and 3 keep only two numbers: nine AI judges worth about two independent ones (Kohli 2026), and collusion in 94% of runs (Shi et al. 2026). Section 2's three pieces of evidence now match Section 1's three failures in order; the third is Amayuelas et al. 2024 (one persuasive agent can sharply cut a group's accuracy). Shu 2026 is cited in Task 2.1.
- Figure 1 sits at the top of page 2, next to the Section 4 overview. RG1 picks the debaters and judge; RG2 settles disputes by debate; the three RG boxes are drawn alike; one Audits box (automatic and human) feeds RG1 and RG2; final decisions feed RG3, which trains the agents.
- A fresh review pass checked consistency against the design rules and rewrote the prose in the PI's voice (fewer semicolons, no staged openers or reflexive contrasts). It also fixed citations in Section 3: Irving et al. and Khan et al. now support standard debate, and Flanigan et al. supports fair panel selection rather than impartial rating.
- The PI qualifications add PeerEval, the AI4Research workshop at AAAI 2025 (co-organizer), and the LLM4SR survey (arXiv:2501.04306). The PeerEval link (peereval-ai.github.io) sits in the reference list, inside the PRD entry, so it costs no space on the four pages.
