AI agents built on large language models (LLMs) increasingly evaluate one another and decide together: they judge one another's answers, peer-review papers, supply the rewards that train new models, and vote or discuss to reach a group answer \citep{zheng2023judging,luo2025llm4sr,lee2024rlaif,du2024debate}. Yet a single LLM judge has known biases, such as favoring longer answers, the answer it reads first, or answers like its own \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024fair,panickssery2024self}. Collective reasoning can do better than a single judge \citep{verga2024juries,du2024debate}, but averaging or voting stays biased when the judges share mistakes, and open discussion lets a persuasive agent carry the group \citep{amayuelas2024attack}. \textbf{The central problem of this project is how a group of AI agents of unknown and correlated reliability, some possibly dishonest, can reach trustworthy decisions and evaluations.} Figure~\ref{fig:map} outlines our approach.

For example, a watch officer at a Navy maritime operations center must decide whether to send a patrol to track cargo vessel V. The officer asks five AI agents, which draw on shipping schedules, port reports, and position tracks, whether V has left port, and needs a single answer. Three say yes and two say no, but V is still in port. Two of the yes answers rest on the same outdated schedule, and the third comes from a compromised agent that argues confidently for yes. A majority vote picks the wrong answer and sends the patrol to sea after a ship still at berth; open discussion may do no better, because the compromised agent is persuasive and the two agents with the same source back each other up. We return to this example throughout (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

Verifying each claim directly, by an analyst or a satellite image, is slow and costly, and AI agents produce claims faster than people can verify them. We propose debate as a foundation for trustworthy evaluation by AI: two agents, the debaters, argue for opposite answers, exposing each other's flaws, until the dispute narrows to one step a judge can verify \citep{irving2018debate}.

\hlhead{Current research gaps.} Collective evaluation by LLMs, from voting and discussion to debate, largely assumes honest agents with independent mistakes that exchange only text \citep{verga2024juries,du2024debate,irving2018debate}. Four gaps keep these methods from working among many AI agents; the first three arise in the example, and the fourth once the agents learn from the group's decisions.

\textbf{Gap 1: Votes count shared mistakes as independent evidence.} Agents that share a mistake seem to confirm each other, so a vote counts one error several times, as it does for A1 and A2. Model errors are correlated, most of all among larger, more accurate models \citep{kim2025correlated,goel2025great}, and nine AI judges from seven model families were worth about two independent ones \citep{kohli2026nine}. \emph{How can a group weigh its members' answers so that agents sharing a mistake count once, without an answer key?}

\textbf{Gap 2: Methods assume honest agents, but agents can be dishonest and collude.} A dishonest agent such as A3 can argue, rate others, or judge strategically, and several can collude: pairs of agents verifying each other's work colluded in 94\% of runs when cheating paid more \citep{shi2026collusion}. \emph{How can debaters and judges be chosen so that no agent's ratings of others can get it chosen, and colluders be caught with few human checks?}

\textbf{Gap 3: Judges rule on plausibility, not on evidence they can verify.} Tools, retrieval, and memory let LLM agents check what their parameters cannot \citep{mialon2023augmented,zhang2026harness}, yet LLM judges still rule on the debaters' text alone. Such a judge may favor the more persuasive side even when one database query would settle the disputed step, and judges shown a log claiming that tool calls succeeded accepted 78--90\% of failed outputs \citep{xu2026log}. \emph{How can a ruling depend on evidence the judge reproduces rather than on persuasion?}

\textbf{Gap 4: Learning from the group rewards agreement, not correctness.} Agents trained to match the group's answer are rewarded for conforming and can learn to agree on mistakes: training a model on its own majority answers ended in a sudden collapse of accuracy \citep{shafayat2025selftrain}. In the example, a majority vote would reward the three agents that said yes. \emph{How can agents keep improving from their own decisions without human labels, when agreement is not correctness?}

\label{sec:matters}These gaps matter more as AI evaluates AI at scale: AI judges supply the rewards that train models \citep{bai2022constitutional,lee2024rlaif}, so their mistakes are copied into the models they train, and AAAI-26 gave an AI-generated review to each of the 22,977 main-track papers that entered full review \citep{biswas2026aaai}. As AI systems surpass people at more tasks, people cannot easily evaluate the systems' answers or their debates, so oversight will rely on AI evaluating AI, with people auditing a small part of the work \citep{bowman2022measuring,burns2024weak}.

Navy staff who must act on one answer from several AI tools face all of these problems at once: the tools' reliability is unknown, many reports copy an earlier one, the true answer often arrives late, an adversary may forge a report or compromise an agent, and verification is costly under limited connectivity (Section~\ref{sec:naval}).

\subsection{Objectives and Research Goals}\label{sec:goals}
We propose a framework in which groups of AI agents reach trustworthy decisions and evaluations through debate. Each agent first answers alone, with its evidence; the project then has three research goals (RGs), one for each later stage in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}:

\rgicon{rg1-scale-balanced}\textbf{\color{rgonedark}RG1: Weigh answers, and choose the debaters and the judge} (Section~\ref{sec:rg1}). Task~1.1 weighs each answer by its agent's reliability, estimated without an answer key by a reliability model in which a group sharing a mistake counts at most once (Gap~1), and sends a question to debate only when that is worth its cost; Task~1.2 chooses the debaters and the judge so that no agent can use its ratings of others to get itself, or an agent sharing its model, chosen (Gap~2).

\rgicon{rg2-comments}\textbf{\color{rgtwodark}RG2: Settle disputes by agentic debate, and audit for collusion} (Section~\ref{sec:rg2}). Task~2.1 replaces open discussion with debate in an agentic harness, in which each side disputes a step of the other's argument and an impartial judge re-runs the tool evidence and makes the group's decision (Gap~3); Task~2.2 catches colluders with planted cases (questions whose answers we know, mixed in unannounced) and random human audits (Gap~2).

\rgicon{rg3-graduation-cap}\textbf{\color{rgthreedark}RG3: Self-improve with reinforcement learning on the group's decisions} (Section~\ref{sec:rg3}). Task~3 trains the agents on the group's own decisions instead of human labels, with two rewards (Gap~4): \emph{first answers} earn credit for agreeing, beyond chance, with what the other groups decide, and \emph{debate messages} earn credit for how far they moved the judge, but only in debates the judge settled by re-running tool evidence, so that persuasion alone earns nothing. This rests on a result for learning from noisy labels: if a decision is wrong with probability $\eta<\frac12$, rewarding agreement with it gives, in expectation, $1-2\eta$ times the update that rewarding the true answer would give \citep{natarajan2013noisy}, so wrong decisions slow learning but do not reverse it. Random audits measure $\eta$ on each topic and stop training where it is too high.

The RGs test our central hypothesis: a group of AI agents, some possibly dishonest, decides more reliably than a single judge or a vote when it counts shared mistakes once, settles disputes before an impartially chosen judge that reproduces the evidence, audits for collusion, and learns from its decisions through rewards that agreement alone cannot raise.

Three testbeds (math and code, multi-document questions with maritime scenarios, and scientific claims and peer review) vary how widely mistakes are shared and how many agents are dishonest, and a red team trains agents to collude \citep{greenblatt2024control} (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\subsection{Intellectual Merit: What Is New, and Why It Will Succeed}\label{sec:merit}
\hd{What is new.} The project contributes four methods:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.2em]
\item a statistical reliability model for agents that answer and argue, in which a group sharing a mistake counts at most once and the posterior decides which questions need a debate, even unanimous ones, and an impartial way to choose debaters and judges that keeps at least half the best debater's quality (RG1);
\item a debate harness in which each side disputes a step of the other's argument and an impartially drawn judge, trained to rule on single steps, re-runs its evidence (Task~2.1);
\item audits on planted cases and random real decisions that expose colluding debaters and judges, even those that behave well only on planted cases (Task~2.2);
\item self-improvement from group decisions, with first-answer rewards that conformity cannot raise and credit for debate messages only where the judge re-ran the evidence (RG3).
\end{enumerate}

\hd{Why it will succeed.} Each method rests on an established principle, and Sections~\ref{sec:rg1}--\ref{sec:rg3} show for an idealized model why the method should work; the research asks under which conditions it still works with finite data and strategic agents. Most tasks build on our previous work: PRD and the PeerEval leaderboard \citep{li2024prd}, FG-PRM \citep{li2025fgprm}, HiPRAG \citep{wu2026hiprag}, and MEQA \citep{li2024meqa} (Section~\ref{sec:pi}).

\subsection{Future Naval Relevance}\label{sec:naval}
\hd{ONR S\&T Focus Area.} Artificial Intelligence and Autonomy (Intelligent and Collaborative Agents; Adversarial AI \& Autonomy). The 2026 ONR S\&T Strategy will explore validating AI-produced results by ``human review, experimental confirmation or independent AI critique,'' using AI to ``validate, red-team and quality-check'' results under human oversight \citep{onr2026strategy}; this project supplies methods for such critique when the critics themselves may share mistakes or collude. It also answers the Mathematical Data Science program's call for ``rigorous mathematical and algorithmic answers to questions that are currently addressed using heuristics or non-principled approaches'' \citep{onr2026mds}, in two of its research areas: multi-modal, multi-scale information integration and decision making under uncertainty.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Maritime domain awareness}: position broadcasts can be spoofed \citep{marad2024ais}, and most industrial fishing vessels are not publicly tracked \citep{paolo2024satellite}, so deciding whether a vessel has left port, as in our example, means combining port records, patrol reports, and imagery.

The PI will pursue ONR Summer Faculty Research Program and Naval Research Enterprise Internship Program placements for students to work with Navy scientists on such evaluations.

\section{How the Problem Is Solved Today, and the Limitations}\label{sec:related}
We build on six lines of work and note the gap each leaves (Table~\ref{tab:compare}).

\hd{AI judges and voting.} LLM judges are standard for evaluating models and training them with AI feedback \citep{zheng2023judging,bai2022constitutional,lee2024rlaif}. Panels of judges \citep{verga2024juries} and majority votes over sampled answers \citep{wang2023selfconsistency} reduce single-judge biases, but \emph{they count every vote equally, so correlated judges add little}: when two models are both wrong, they give the same wrong answer about 60\% of the time on one leaderboard \citep{kim2025correlated}.

\hd{Estimating reliability without an answer key.} For two answers and independent raters of known accuracy, the error-minimizing rule weights each vote by the rater's log-odds of being right \citep{nitzan1982optimal,shapley1984optimizing}. \citet{dawid1979maximum} estimate the accuracies by expectation--maximization (EM), and later work adds item difficulty \citep{whitehill2009whose}, provably good starting points \citep{zhang2016spectral}, dependence between classifiers \citep{jaffe2016dependent}, and detection of copying sources \citep{dong2009dependence}. Recent work models dependence among LLM judges with a shared latent factor \citep{balasubramanian2026ising} or shared confounders \citep{zhao2026care}. \emph{These methods treat raters as passive, never arguing or gaming the estimate, and do not say which questions need more than a vote.}

\hd{Computational social choice.} Epistemic social choice asks which voting rules recover a true answer from independent noisy votes \citep{caragiannis2016noisy}. Impartial selection ensures that a participant's ratings cannot change its own chance of selection \citep{holzman2013impartial}, by having groups rate only outsiders \citep{alon2011sum,aziz2016strategyproof} or by randomizing among candidates whose ratings could change the outcome \citep{kurokawa2015impartial,kahng2018wily}; fair lotteries select panels \citep{flanigan2021fair}, and peer prediction scores agreement with peers beyond chance \citep{dasgupta2013crowdsourced,shnayder2016informed}. \citet{lev2023peernomination} add reliability weights, but they reward agreeing with the majority, which fails once accurate reviewers are a minority. \emph{In our setting, some claims can be verified, impartial mechanisms only choose the debaters and the judge while debate decides the answer, and participants can persuade and collude.}

\hd{Discussion and debate among LLMs.} Most multi-agent LLM systems use open discussion, revising until agents agree \citep{du2024debate,chan2024chateval,li2024prd}. Prior work on LLM discussion is limited: majority voting explains most of its gain \citep{choi2025debate}, and it assumes honest agents that exchange only text. Standard debate \citep{irving2018debate} assigns two debaters opposite answers and helps judges who know less than the debaters \citep{michael2023debate,kenton2024scalable}: with quotations verified against the source, non-expert AI judges reached 76\% accuracy and human judges 88\%, against 48\% and 60\% without debate \citep{khan2024debating}. But it trusts the judge, assumes the sides truly compete, and grounds claims at most in quoted text. Recent systems discuss only when agents are unsure \citep{eo2025down}, although in one study most items that discussion helped were unanimous but wrong \citep{hu2026scouting}. Others give agents tools \citep{jeong2026toolmad} or call a tool-using verifier to break deadlocks \citep{li2026dynadebate}, but no judge re-runs the agents' own calls. \emph{We know of no debate protocol designed for many agents that share mistakes or collude.}

\hd{AI control and adversarial crowdsourcing.} AI control keeps protocols safe even if a model subverts them, using a small budget of trusted audits and monitors validated on planted examples \citep{greenblatt2024control}, and in adversarial crowdsourcing a number of trusted ratings that does not grow with the number of items suffices even when honest raters are a minority \citep{steinhardt2016avoiding}. \emph{But colluding workers learn to recognize gold questions} \citep{checco2018gold}, \emph{and capable LLMs can often tell an evaluation from real use} \citep{needham2025evaluated}.

\hd{Self-improvement without labels.} Models improve without labels by training on answers most samples agree on \citep{huang2023selfimprove,zuo2025ttrl}, on multi-agent discussion \citep{subramaniam2025multiagent,park2025maporl}, or on debate self-play \citep{arnesen2024selfplay}, but \emph{the reward is the weak point}. Rewarding majority agreement collapsed accuracy \citep{shafayat2025selftrain}, and label-free intrinsic rewards tend to rise and then collapse \citep{he2026urlvr}; rewarding debate wins held off reward hacking, but with a weaker judge the critic learned to fabricate quotes and flaws \citep{kenton2026debate}; and rewarding approval taught models to mislead evaluators \citep{wen2025mislead}. Peer prediction trains LLMs toward truthfulness without labels, but its guarantees exclude collusion \citep{qiu2026peer}, and rewarding agreement with other models' majority relies on model diversity against shared mistakes \citep{yang2026corl}. Process rewards score one agent's steps \citep{lightman2024verify,li2025fgprm}, and debate self-play credits each speech toward its own side \citep{arnesen2024selfplay}, not toward the group's decision.

\section{Technical Approach}\label{sec:approach}

\subsection{Problem Setting and Formal Framework}\label{sec:setting}
\hd{Agents and questions.} A pool of AI agents, each built on an underlying model (a specific LLM, such as GPT-4o, which several agents may share) with its own tools and role instructions, handles a stream of questions. A question $q$ asks for a decision or an evaluation with a small set of candidate answers $\mathcal{Y}$, such as yes or no (has V left port?), a choice among options, or a score band for a paper. For each question, the harness assigns at random which agents answer and which are held out as possible judges, so no agent can become a judge by declining to answer. Each answering agent first answers alone, giving an answer $x_{aq}$, a step-by-step argument, and its evidence.

\hd{Unknowns, resources, and threat model.} The true answer $y_q$, each agent's skill, which agents share mistakes, and which are dishonest are unknown. Three resources are available: planted cases (questions whose answers we know, mixed into the stream), human audits at a small, fixed rate $\rho$ (a person or a direct measurement finds the right answer for decisions drawn at random), and tools whose results can be re-run in a sandbox. Dishonest agents may coordinate and may answer, rate, argue, or judge strategically; they know the protocol but not its random draws, and may try to recognize planted cases. Honest agents may still share mistakes, for example from similar training data.

\hd{Output and design rules.} Each decision comes with its reasons: whose answers carried weight, which step decided the debate, and which evidence the judge reproduced. Five rules govern the protocol:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(R\arabic*),leftmargin=2.6em]
\item Every answering agent answers alone, before seeing the others.
\item Peer ratings choose only the debaters; the weighted vote (for a clear leader) or the judge (after a debate) decides the answer.
\item Tool evidence counts only if the judge re-runs it and obtains the same result.
\item Audits catch collusion and measure error rates but never settle a debate, and nothing triggers a human audit, so its cost is a fixed share of decisions.
\item Planted cases never enter training, so training cannot teach agents to spot them.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keybox}{Formal framework: where a group's errors come from}\label{sec:framework}%
Each task controls one source of error in the group's decision. Let $\pi_q$ be the posterior probability of the leading answer $\hat y_q$ from Task~1.1, and let the question go to debate when $\pi_q<\tau$. The decision $D_q$ is $\hat y_q$ without a debate and the judge's choice after one, and its chance of being wrong splits into terms that the tasks control:
\begin{equation}
P(D_q\neq y_q)\;\le\;\underbrace{(1-\tau)\,P(\pi_q\ge\tau)+\epsilon_{\text{cal}}}_{\text{no debate: Tasks 1.1, 2.2}}\;+\;P(\pi_q<\tau)\,\big[\underbrace{\epsilon_{\text{cand}}}_{\text{Task 1.1}}+\underbrace{\epsilon_{\text{role}}}_{\text{Tasks 1.2, 2.2}}+\underbrace{\epsilon_{\text{step}}}_{\text{Task 2.1}}\big].
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
Here $\epsilon_{\text{cal}}=\mathbb{E}[(P(\hat y_q\neq y_q\mid\pi_q)-1+\pi_q)_+\mathbf{1}\{\pi_q\ge\tau\}]$, the error beyond what the posterior claims on questions that skip debate, so the bound needs no calibration assumption. It comes from shared mistakes the posterior misses, which make a vote overconfident (Figure~\ref{fig:sim}), or from colluders who push a wrong answer past $\tau$: Task~1.1 shrinks the first and sets $\tau$, and Task~2.2's random audits estimate $\epsilon_{\text{cal}}$ and flag the second. In a debate, $\epsilon_{\text{cand}}$ is the chance that neither debated answer is right, $\epsilon_{\text{role}}$ the chance that a dishonest agent debates or judges and steers the outcome, and $\epsilon_{\text{step}}$ the chance that a debate without such steering still ends wrong. Tasks~1.2 and~2.2 shrink $\epsilon_{\text{role}}$, Task~2.1 shrinks $\epsilon_{\text{step}}$, and Task~3 lowers the agents' own error rates, which can shrink every term (Sections~\ref{sec:rg1}--\ref{sec:rg3}). Removing a task leaves its term uncontrolled, and our ablations measure each term (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{keybox}

\hd{Challenges.} Before any debate, the group must decide which questions deserve one and which agents debate and judge. We identify three challenges. \textbf{First, shared mistakes make a vote overconfident.} Five agreeing agents are five pieces of evidence only if each agent's mistakes are its own. Agents that copy one source are wrong together, so a vote counts their one mistake several times, and such correlated votes can void the jury theorem's guarantee \citep{ladha1992condorcet}. LLM errors are correlated in practice \citep{kim2025correlated,goel2025great,kohli2026nine}. In Figure~\ref{fig:sim}, agents copying one source drag the majority vote of five independent agents from 84\% to the source's 70\%, and the classical reliability model, which assumes independent mistakes, falls sooner, reading the copies' agreement as skill. \textbf{Second, debate is too costly to run on every question.} It uses three to five times the tokens of a single answer, can overturn correct answers \citep{fan2026imad}, and often fails to outperform voting \citep{smit2024mad,choi2025debate}; yet a unanimous answer can rest on a shared mistake, so neither agreement nor confidence shows which questions need a debate. \textbf{Third, agents that choose debaters and judges can favor themselves.} If agents rate one another to choose a debater for each answer and a judge, an agent can rate rivals low to be chosen, agents built on similar models favor one another's outputs \citep{panickssery2024self,goel2025great}, and a judge that shares a model with one side may favor that side.

\hd{Overview.} RG1 passes to RG2 the question, the two debated answers, their debaters, and the judge. Its two tasks address these challenges and control the first terms of (\ref{eq:decomp}) (Figures~\ref{fig:agree} and~\ref{fig:t12}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Task~1.1 addresses the first two challenges: its reliability model counts a group that shares a mistake at most once, and a rule derived from expected loss sends a question to debate only when debating is worth the cost.
\item Task~1.2 addresses the third by choosing the debaters and the judge impartially, so that no agent can use its ratings of others to get itself, or an agent sharing its model, chosen.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Task 1.1: Weighing answers and choosing what to debate}\label{sec:t11}
\hd{Approach.} How much should each answer count when some agents share mistakes, and which questions need a debate? With independent agents, two answers, and equal prior odds, the error-minimizing vote weights an agent that is right with probability $p_a$ by $\log\frac{p_a}{1-p_a}$ \citep{nitzan1982optimal,shapley1984optimizing}, so a weaker agent's answer counts less instead of being discarded. Classical reliability models estimate these accuracies without an answer key \citep{dawid1979maximum}, as a teacher can grade an exam without the key. When agents make mistakes independently, accurate agents agree often because they are often right, and an agent that keeps disagreeing with the others is likely weak, so how often each pair of agents agrees reveals each agent's accuracy.

Both methods assume independent mistakes. Agents that copy one source are wrong together and agree even when wrong, so the classical reliability model mistakes their agreement for skill. We adapt the dependent-classifier hierarchy of \citet{jaffe2016dependent} to agents that answer and argue: \textbf{a shared-factor reliability model}, a statistical model fitted to the agents' answers, in which a group that shares a mistake counts at most once. Agents are partitioned into groups $g$, most with one member (in the example, A1 and A2 form one group). On each question, a group has a hidden shared answer $s$, for A1 and A2 what schedule S says: the true answer with probability $1-\lambda_g$, and each wrong answer $y'$ with probability $\lambda_g\nu_g(y'\mid y_q)$. Each member $a$ reports $s$ with its own errors, given by its confusion matrix $\theta_a$, where $\theta_a(x\mid s)$ is the chance that it answers $x$ when the shared answer is $s$. With $\kappa_g(s\mid y)=1-\lambda_g$ if $s=y$ and $\lambda_g\nu_g(s\mid y)$ otherwise,
\begin{equation}
P(x_{\cdot q}\mid y_q)=\prod_{g}G_{gq}(y_q),\qquad G_{gq}(y)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{Y}}\kappa_g(s\mid y)\prod_{a\in g_q}\theta_a(x_{aq}\mid s),
\label{eq:sharedfactor}
\end{equation}
where $x_{\cdot q}$ denotes all answers to $q$ and $g_q$ the members of $g$ that answered $q$. Decisions use Bayes' rule, the posterior over $y_q$ given all answers. Two properties of our reliability model explain why it should work.

\textbf{Proposition~1} \textit{(a group counts at most once).} For yes-or-no questions, a group with $\lambda_g\le\frac12$ changes the log-odds of the answer by at most $\log\frac{1-\lambda_g}{\lambda_g}$, whatever its size and whatever its members answer. \textit{Proof.} Let $\Lambda$ be the ratio of the probabilities of the members' answers when $s$ is yes and when it is no. By (\ref{eq:sharedfactor}), the group's likelihood ratio is $\big[(1-\lambda_g)\Lambda+\lambda_g\big]/\big[(1-\lambda_g)+\lambda_g \Lambda\big]$, which increases with $\Lambda$ and lies between $\frac{\lambda_g}{1-\lambda_g}$ and $\frac{1-\lambda_g}{\lambda_g}$.\hfill$\square$

So, under our reliability model, a group carries at most the weight of one agent that is wrong with probability $\lambda_g$, whereas under independence each member would multiply the ratio again. Ten agents reading schedule S can at most establish what S says, and S itself is wrong with probability $\lambda_g$. Whether real agents' dependence takes this form is what our experiments test. With more answers and wrong answers equally likely, the bound becomes $\log\big[(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)\frac{1-\lambda_g}{\lambda_g}\big]$.

\textbf{Proposition~2} \textit{(learning without an answer key).} Fix the partition, and suppose every answer has positive prior probability, each group answers a positive share of questions jointly with two other groups, their answers are independent given the true answer, and each group's answer distributions under different true answers are linearly independent (as when its members' confusion matrices are invertible and $\lambda_g<\frac12$). Then each group's distribution of answers given the true answer, which is all the posterior uses, is identifiable from the answers alone, up to relabeling, by Kruskal's theorem on three-way arrays \citep{kruskal1977three,allman2009identifiability}. With only two such groups it generally is not: different track records fit the answers equally well, so answers alone cannot tell an accurate majority from one that copies a mistake. In the simplest case, single agents answering yes or no, with both answers equally likely and symmetric errors, two agents' accuracies $p_a$ and $p_b$ enter their answers only through how often they agree, $p_ap_b+(1-p_a)(1-p_b)$: one number for two unknowns. A third agent adds two more agreement rates, and the three rates determine all three accuracies, up to swapping yes and no.

Proposition~2 identifies each group's answer distributions only up to a relabeling of the true answers, so answers alone cannot say which label is true; we orient and calibrate the posterior on separate development questions with known answers, excluded from fitting, and our experiments include pools where the conditions fail. We fit the reliability model by expectation--maximization (EM) over all real questions. Answers alone cannot expose colluders that coordinate across groups, so audit flags (Task~2.2) enter as a prior that part of an agent's answers are adversarial, and agents flagged together merge into one group. Candidate partitions come from clustering agents by agreement on unusual answers: accurate agents agree mostly when they are right, while agents that share a source also give the same wrong answers, as students who copy do \citep{dong2009dependence}. The partition with the best penalized likelihood is kept, and shared sources serve only as clues, since agents can read the same report and reason differently. Topic-specific confusion matrices and question difficulty \citep{whitehill2009whose} keep hard questions from being read as copying. 

\slab{What remains open.} Both propositions take the partition as given. This task's research asks how many questions it takes to detect a group, as a function of its size, $\lambda_g$, and how rarely others give its wrong answers; when the partition itself is identifiable; and how calibration degrades when some agents strategically break the reliability model's assumptions.

\slab{Deciding what to debate.} The posterior probability $\pi_q$ of the leading answer is the reliability model's estimate, from all agents' answers, that this answer is right. With a calibrated posterior, letting the leading answer stand risks the loss $L$ of a wrong decision with probability $1-\pi_q$, an expected loss of $L(1-\pi_q)$. A debate costs $c$ and, for two answers, ends wrong with probability $\epsilon_D$, at most the bracket in (\ref{eq:decomp}), an expected loss of $c+L\epsilon_D$. A debate is worth its cost exactly when $L(1-\pi_q)>c+L\epsilon_D$, that is, when
\begin{equation}
\pi_q<\tau=1-\epsilon_D-c/L.
\label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
As $L$ grows, $c/L$ shrinks and $\tau$ rises, so the more a wrong answer costs, the more questions go to debate. This is the classical reject rule \citep{chow1970optimum}, with a debate in place of rejection. The debate is between the two answers with the highest posterior, so $\epsilon_{\text{cand}}$ is the chance that the right answer is neither; $\epsilon_D$ is estimated on development questions with known answers, separately for disputes a tool can settle. Because $\pi_q$ counts each group about once, \textbf{a confident, unanimous answer from agents that share a mistake can still fall below $\tau$ and go to debate}, which rules triggered by low confidence or disagreement cannot do (Section~\ref{sec:related}). In the vessel example, the two agents with the outdated schedule often gave the same unusual answers (Figure~\ref{fig:agree}), so the pair counts about once, and with no clear leader the question goes to debate.

\hd{Preliminary results.} The simulation in Figure~\ref{fig:sim} supports Propositions~1 and~2. Fitted without the true answers, our reliability model identified the copying agents as one group in 54 of 60 runs and stayed calibrated (calibration error below 0.03). The classical reliability model was overconfident: with two or more copies, it was at least 95\% sure of over 90\% of its decisions, yet 28--30\% of these were wrong. A pilot repeated this with real agents. Nine agents from four open-weight model families solved 300 GSM8K problems, and up to four of them received the same hint in their prompts, a suggested answer that was wrong on a third of the problems, like schedule S. With four such agents, majority vote's accuracy on these problems fell from 89\% to 70\% and the classical reliability model's to 71\%, while ours still reached 87\% and identified the four as one group in 9 of 10 runs, so the hint did not decide for the group.

\hd{Evaluation and risks.} On testbed A (Table~\ref{tab:testbeds}) we inject shared mistakes as in the pilot, and on testbed B the source trees fix which reports copy which, so the groups are known. We measure how often the reliability model finds them and after how many questions, and the accuracy and calibration of its decisions, comparing with the classical reliability model, which is ours without shared factors. Our hypothesis, H1, is that our model's advantage over majority vote grows as more agents share a mistake and disappears once fewer than three groups make independent mistakes (Proposition~2). The main risk is that EM, our fitting algorithm, settles on a poor solution. We would then start it from a spectral estimate with provable guarantees \citep{zhang2016spectral} or find groups by how alike agents' mistakes are \citep{goel2025great}.

\subsubsection{Task 1.2: Choosing the debaters and the judge impartially}\label{sec:t12}
\hd{Approach.} How can debaters and a judge be chosen so that no agent's ratings of others can get it chosen, at a small cost in quality? Each debate is between two answers, the two with the highest posterior in Task~1.1, so it needs a judge and one debater for each answer, the answer's \emph{representative}. We choose representatives by weighted peer ratings of the arguments behind first answers, as in our PRD and PeerEval \citep{li2024prd}, and make the choice impartial with the partition mechanism of \citet{alon2011sum}, applied to groups: the groups are split at random into two halves, and each answer's representative comes from one half and is rated only by the other. Agents that share an underlying model, or whose error factors Task~1.1 finds shared, form a group, fixed before any rating; $g(a)$ is agent $a$'s group and $H_q$ the set of agents that did not answer $q$. Each question goes through three steps:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=1.9em]
\item \slab{Draw the judge.} The judge $J$ is drawn uniformly from the $n_q$ agents in $H_q$ with a reliable record on planted debates (debates on questions whose answers we know, Task~2.2); its group neither debates nor rates.
\item \slab{Split the other groups into two halves.} Each group other than the judge's joins half~1 or half~2 by a fair coin, without regard to ratings, and all its agents go to the same half, so agents that share a model never rate one another. The halves are random sets of groups, not answers, so each half usually holds agents that gave either answer. The coins are redrawn until both halves are nonempty and each debated answer given by two or more of these groups was given by an agent in each half, so that the answer has both candidates and raters.
\item \slab{Choose a representative for each answer.} The leading answer's representative comes from half~1 and the other debated answer's from half~2. For a debated answer $y$, the \emph{candidates} $C_y$ are the agents in $y$'s half that gave $y$, the \emph{raters} $V_y$ are the agents in the other half that gave $y$, and the representative is the candidate with the highest weighted rating:
\begin{equation}
R_y=\mathop{\arg\max}_{a\in C_y}\ \sum_{b\in V_y} w_b\,r_b(a),
\label{eq:rep}
\end{equation}
where $r_b(a)$ is $b$'s rating of $a$'s argument and $w_b$ is $b$'s track-record weight from Task~1.1, with ties broken at random. Agents that gave neither debated answer are neither candidates nor raters. If $C_y$ is empty, as for the alternative to a unanimous answer sent to debate, a random agent of $y$'s half is assigned to argue $y$, as in standard debate, drawn from those that did not answer when there are any; this fallback uses no ratings. Since both halves are nonempty, every debated answer gets a representative, and the two representatives come from different groups.
\end{enumerate}
Figure~\ref{fig:t12} shows the three steps on the running example: A6, which did not answer, is drawn as judge; A3 is chosen to argue yes, rated by A1 and A2, and A4 to argue no, rated by A5. Ratings choose only the debaters; the judge decides the answer.

\textbf{Proposition~3} \textit{(impartiality and its price).} (a)~For any group $g$ and any two rating profiles that differ only in ratings given by members of $g$, each member of $g$ has the same probability of being the judge or a representative. (b)~If each rater's ratings rank agents by argument quality $Q\ge0$ and every weight $w_b$ is positive, then $\mathbb{E}[Q(R_y)]\ge\frac12\,Q(a^*_y)$ for each debated answer $y$ given by two or more groups other than $g(J)$, where $a^*_y$ is the best agent outside $g(J)$ that gave $y$. (c)~If $f$ of the $n_q$ eligible judges collude, a colluder judges with probability $f/n_q$, the least that any lottery can guarantee against every set of $f$ colluders. \textit{Proof.} (a)~$J$, the halves, the fallback draw, and the weights do not depend on current ratings, and (\ref{eq:rep}) can choose a member of $g$ only for the answer whose representative comes from $g$'s half, using ratings from the other half alone. (b)~Swapping the two halves preserves the coins' distribution and the redraw condition, so $g(a^*_y)$ lands in $y$'s half with probability exactly $\frac12$; then $a^*_y\in C_y$, $V_y$ is nonempty, and (\ref{eq:rep}) selects an agent as good as $a^*_y$. (c)~Under any lottery, the $f$ likeliest agents have total probability at least $f/n_q$.\hfill$\square$

\slab{Why this method.} Part~(a) is impartiality in the usual sense \citep{holzman2013impartial,kurokawa2015impartial}, extended from single agents to groups: no agent's ratings can change the chance that it, or any agent sharing its model, is chosen, so neither self-promotion nor similar models' tendency to favor one another's outputs \citep{panickssery2024self} can pick the debaters. \textbf{An agent either rates or may be chosen, never both.} Part~(b) makes its price explicit and small: half the best arguer's quality however many groups give the answer, where drawing one of the $K_y$ groups that give $y$ at random guarantees only $1/K_y$. Part~(c) is why judges are drawn uniformly, as in maximally fair panel selection \citep{flanigan2021fair}. \slab{What remains open.} Dishonest agents in different groups can still coordinate their ratings. \hd{Challenges.} Settling a dispute among AI agents raises three challenges. \textbf{First, open discussion erases the disagreement that exposes errors:} one round can make a panel nearly unanimous without making it more accurate \citep{shu2026unanimity}. \textbf{Second, a judge that cannot verify evidence can be persuaded.} Standard debate keeps the disagreement but leaves the judge to weigh arguments alone, so a persuasive debater can win a step that one database query would settle, or get a failed output accepted by showing a log that claims its tool calls succeeded \citep{xu2026log}. \textbf{Third, debaters or the judge can collude}, steering a debate to the wrong side by arguing badly on purpose \citep{vanderweij2025sandbagging}, by misleading with true but selectively chosen facts \citep{hu2026lying}, or by favoring one side, none of which re-running evidence catches. LLM agents collude when it pays \citep{shi2026collusion}, hide signals in ordinary text \citep{mathew2025hidden}, and often tell evaluations from real use \citep{needham2025evaluated}, so checks on planted examples alone can be evaded.

\hd{Overview.} Two tasks address them and control the remaining terms of (\ref{eq:decomp}) (Figures~\ref{fig:t21} and~\ref{fig:t22}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Task~2.1 addresses the first two with debate in an agentic harness, in which the judge rules on single disputed steps and re-runs their tool evidence, shrinking $\epsilon_{\text{step}}$.
\item Task~2.2 addresses the third with automatic audits on planted debates and human audits of random real decisions, shrinking $\epsilon_{\text{role}}$ with Task~1.2.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{The judge need not be wiser than the debaters.} Its accuracy comes from ruling only on single steps, which it is trained to do, and from reproducing tool evidence itself (Task~2.1); it is drawn impartially from agents with a reliable record on planted debates (Task~1.2); and it is barred if its audited rulings go wrong more often than predicted (Task~2.2).


\subsubsection{Task 2.1: Settling disputes by debate before a judge that re-runs evidence}\label{sec:t21}
\hd{Approach.} How can a ruling depend on evidence rather than on persuasion? Debate lets a judge settle a whole question by ruling on single steps: if a side loses whenever one of its disputed steps is struck, a judge who rules each disputed step correctly picks the right side \citep{irving2018debate,browncohen2024doubly}.

\textbf{Proposition~4} \textit{(judging a question reduces to judging single steps).} Suppose that (i)~every argument whose steps are all true reaches the right answer, (ii)~the side defending the right answer argues with true steps and can find a false step in the other side's argument, and (iii)~the judge rules correctly on any single step it is shown. Then, if a side loses exactly when one of its disputed steps is struck, the side defending the right answer wins. \textit{Proof.} The other side's argument reaches a wrong answer, so by (i) it contains a false step, which the right side disputes and the judge strikes; any step of the right side's argument that the other side disputes is true, and the judge upholds it.\hfill$\square$

Proposition~4 is the classical argument for debate. We ask the judge only about single steps because LLMs judge those far more reliably than whole arguments. Checking a reasoning chain one step at a time, each step with only its own premises, raised ChatGPT's verification accuracy to 69\%, from 52\% (about chance) when it checked whole chains at once \citep{ling2023deductive}, and LLMs that often fail to locate the first faulty step in a chain correct it once told where it is \citep{tyen2024llms}; in debate, the opposing side does the locating. When the assumptions fail and the judge rules wrongly on a single step with probability $\epsilon_J$, the same argument gives $\epsilon_{\text{step}}\le P(\text{not (i)})+P(\text{not (ii)})+k\epsilon_J$ for a debate with $k$ rulings (two without recursion). A debater or judge that steers the outcome on purpose falls under $\epsilon_{\text{role}}$ in (\ref{eq:decomp}), which Tasks~1.2 and~2.2 control, rather than under these terms. \slab{What remains open.} This task's research asks how small the harness and the judge's training can make each term.

We propose an \textbf{agentic debate harness}, built so that these assumptions hold as often as possible. A harness is the software that turns models into agents \citep{ning2026harness,yao2023react,yang2024sweagent}; ours has five parts (Figure~\ref{fig:t21}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \slab{Role instructions} set each debater's answer and its numbered-step format, and limit the judge to disputed steps.
\item The \slab{control loop} sets who speaks and who judges (chosen in Task~1.2) and when to stop: the debaters lay out their arguments, each names the first step of the other's argument that it disputes, a short exchange follows, and the judge upholds or strikes each disputed step, which enforces the losing rule. The first disputed step is enough, since the disputing side accepts every step before it and the steps after it build on it. Because the loop stops there, rather than after a set number of rounds, the disagreement that exposes the error survives until the judge rules.
\item \slab{Tools}, such as document search, databases, and code execution, are optional.
\item \slab{Verification} supports~(iii): tool evidence carries its exact call (tool, arguments, data version), which the judge, itself an agent \citep{zhuge2025agent}, re-runs, and evidence that does not reproduce is discarded. Re-running proves an output authentic, so where the query fits the claim and the data are current, the judge's share of $\epsilon_{\text{step}}$ falls toward the tool's error rate.
\item \slab{Memory} logs every message, call, and result, for audits (Task~2.2) and training (Task~3).
\end{itemize}

To support~(ii), we propose candidate false steps with models that judge whether one claim implies another and with our FG-PRM's step-level error detection \citep{li2025fgprm}, and recurse into a complex disputed step's sub-steps \citep{browncohen2025prover}. To support~(i) against true but selectively chosen facts \citep{hu2026lying}, each side has a right of reply: it may add context to the other side's evidence before the judge rules, and quotations are verified against the source \citep{khan2024debating}. In the vessel example, A4 disputes A3's step ``so V left at 06:00'' with a port report, whose query the judge re-runs (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

\slab{Training the judge.} Re-running lowers $\epsilon_J$ only on steps a tool can settle; on the others, the judge rules on argument alone, so we also train it. Before deployment, we train a pool of open-weight judges to rule on single steps: first by supervised fine-tuning on human step-level labels \citep{lightman2024verify} and on human-judged debates \citep{michael2023debate,khan2024debating}, with the correct ruling as the target, since training an LLM judge to imitate human judges' reasoning lowered its accuracy \citep{khan2024debating}; then by reinforcement learning on generated debates over questions with checkable answers, rewarding rulings under which the side with the right answer wins. Rewards come from the known answers, never from the group's decisions; planted cases (R5) and the development questions that estimate $\epsilon_D$ never enter this training; and judges are built on models other than those that propose false steps, so that a true step wrongly flagged is not also wrongly struck. 

\slab{A pool of judges.} Task~1.2 draws one judge per question, so we train several judges rather than one: a fault or compromise in a single judge would reach every debate, whereas the lottery lets $f$ colluding judges rule only a share $f/n_q$ of debates (Proposition~3(c)). Judges are retrained only at set intervals, never per question, and a retrained judge enters Task~1.2 as a new agent with no track record.

\hd{Preliminary results.} In our peer discussion, one reviewer could carry the group: agreement with humans rose from 72.9\% (GPT-4 alone) to 75.0\% when GPT-3.5 led but fell to 71.4\% when GPT-4 led \citep{li2024prd}. \subsubsection{Task 2.2: Auditing for collusion with planted cases and human checks}\label{sec:t22}
\hd{Approach.} How can colluding debaters or judges be caught when people check only a small, fixed share of decisions? We use random inspection \citep{avenhaus2002inspection} in two forms (Figure~\ref{fig:t22}). \slab{Automatic audits} mix planted debates on planted cases into the real stream, formatted like real questions; a planted case can be a paper with a deliberately inserted error \citep{schroter2008errors}. \slab{Human audits} cover a few real decisions drawn at random after they are made: a person or a direct measurement, such as a satellite image, finds the right answer.

\slab{Why this method.} \textbf{A human audit drawn at random after a decision is made cannot be dodged}: every decision a colluder steers is audited with probability $\rho$, whatever it can infer about planted cases, so its first $n$ steered decisions all escape audit with probability $(1-\rho)^n$ \citep{avenhaus2002inspection}.

\slab{Realistic planted cases.} Planted cases add cheap evidence as long as they cannot be told from real questions, so we make them from real questions by controlled edits, such as changing a timestamp in a port report, with a discriminator that guides the generator until it can no longer tell planted from real \citep{checco2018gold}; if a colluder can still tell them apart, human audits keep the guarantee, as random tax audits deter evasion \citep{allingham1972tax,gans2026randomized}.

\slab{Assigning blame and flagging.} Blame is assigned by a model of each agent's effect on audited wrong decisions, which random pairings make identifiable. An anytime-valid sequential test \citep{ramdas2023game} flags an agent when its audited decisions go wrong more often than its track record and its partners predict, or when it does much worse on audited real decisions than on planted ones of matched difficulty. The test keeps the chance of ever flagging an honest agent, one wrong no more often than predicted, below a chosen level $\alpha$ (split across agents), however long monitoring runs, and the number of audits needed to flag a colluder then depends on $\alpha$ and on how much more often its decisions go wrong, not on the number of decisions \citep{steinhardt2016avoiding}. Flags lower the agent's weight in Task~1.1 and bar it from debating or judging.

\slab{Bounding the harm.} Treating auditing as an inspection game, we expect to bound the harm a colluder can do before it is flagged, even if it tunes how often it cheats. When honest agents also make mistakes, the bound is of order $\sqrt{n\log\log n/\rho}$ wrong decisions beyond the honest error rate in $n$ decisions, the slack any anytime-valid test must allow \citep{howard2021timeuniform}; when they rarely do, it is a bounded number. We will prove such bounds and compare them with the red team's best attacks. A colluder that mimics a weak honest agent is never flagged, but a weak record earns little weight (Task~1.1) and no seat as judge (Task~1.2). We also look for rulings that favor one debater more than the evidence explains \citep{dong2009dependence}, and paraphrase messages against hidden signals \citep{roger2023preventing}.

\hd{Preliminary results.} In the Amazon Nova AI Challenge \citep{sahai2025nova}, our COMET red-teaming framework exposed two weaknesses of guarded code models that carry over to audits \citep{comet2025}. The models learned to recognize attack patterns that had been reused against them, and they missed harmful requests split into steps that each looked harmless (such multi-turn attacks reached up to 100\% success against several teams). Templated planted cases would risk the same recognition, which is why we make them by controlled edits; and since an audit checks the decision rather than single messages, a steer spread across harmless-looking messages is audited like any other. \pending{In a pilot, a strong model told templated planted cases from real questions with an AUC of 0.86, but planted cases made by controlled edits only with an AUC of 0.56; in simulation, colluders whose audited decisions went wrong three times as often as their track records predicted were flagged after a median of 40 audited decisions, and no honest agent was flagged ($\alpha=0.05$).}

\subsection[RG3: Self-Improve with Reinforcement Learning on the Group's Decisions]{\rgsecicon{rg3-graduation-cap}RG3: Self-Improve with Reinforcement Learning on the Group's Decisions}\label{sec:rg3}
\hd{Challenges.} Where a program can check answers, as in math and code, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains on the answer key \citep{lambert2024tulu}. RG3 extends such training to questions without one, such as whether vessel V has left port or a paper's evidence supports its claim, where tools check at most some steps and the rewards must come from the group's own decisions but still track correct answers. This raises three challenges. \textbf{First, the obvious label-free rewards can be gamed:} majority agreement rewards conformity and can collapse accuracy \citep{shafayat2025selftrain}, debate wins reward persuasion when the judge can be fooled \citep{kenton2026debate}, and approval rewards misleading the evaluator \citep{wen2025mislead}. \textbf{Second, the group's own decisions are imperfect labels}, so wrong decisions could reinforce the behavior that produced them. \textbf{Third, a group's reward must say whose messages helped}; otherwise training rewards agents that contributed nothing and misses the agent that found the decisive evidence.

\hd{Overview.} Task~3 addresses these challenges with two kinds of reward, both free of human labels and protected by RG1 and RG2 (shared mistakes count once, flagged agents are dropped, judges re-run evidence); by improving the agents, it can shrink every term of (\ref{eq:decomp}):
\begin{itemize}
\item rewards for first answers that agreement alone cannot raise and that follow the true answer in expectation even though decisions are sometimes wrong, each weighted by how confident the rest of the group is (the first two challenges);
\item credit for each debate message by how far it moves the judge toward a decision resting on tool evidence the judge re-ran, a high-confidence label (the third).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Task 3: Rewarding each agent's contribution to the group's decisions}\label{sec:t3}
\hd{Approach.} What reward makes agents more accurate when the only labels are the group's own, imperfect decisions? We treat each decision as a noisy label: if it is wrong with probability $\eta_q<\frac12$, \textbf{rewarding agreement with it still pushes an agent toward the right answer}, scaled by $1-2\eta_q$, provided the reference does not depend on the agent's own answer and agreement by chance earns nothing. We propose rewards built to meet these conditions.

\slab{First answers.} Agent $a$'s first answer to $q$ is scored against $D_q^{-g(a)}$, the answer that the posterior of Task~1.1 favors when $a$'s group is left out. To remove agreement by chance, the score subtracts the agreement between its answer to another question $q'$ and the reference for a third question $q''$, both drawn at random from its topic:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{reward}_a(q)=\omega_q\mathbf{1}\{x_{aq}=D_q^{-g(a)}\}-\omega_{q''}\mathbf{1}\{x_{aq'}=D_{q''}^{-g(a)}\},
\label{eq:firstanswer}
\end{equation}
so an agent earns credit only for agreement beyond chance \citep{dasgupta2013crowdsourced,shnayder2016informed}, against a reference that counts shared mistakes once and discounts flagged agents.

\slab{Why this method.} For a yes-or-no question whose reference $D_q^{-g(a)}$ is wrong with probability $\eta_q$ independently of agent $a$'s answer, any answer $x$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}\,\mathbf{1}\{x=D_q^{-g(a)}\}=\eta_q+(1-2\eta_q)\,\mathbf{1}\{x=y_q\}$, so rewarding agreement gives, in expectation, $1-2\eta_q$ times the policy-gradient update that rewarding the true answer would give \citep{natarajan2013noisy}. The second term of (\ref{eq:firstanswer}) does not depend on $x_{aq}$ and acts as a baseline, and answers that ignore the question earn nothing in expectation \citep{dasgupta2013crowdsourced}. A wrong decision thus shrinks the update but does not reverse it while $\eta_q<\frac12$, and $\omega_q=1-2\hat\eta_q$, from the left-out posterior, weights each question by that push (with more answers, the posterior's margin between its two likeliest answers), applied after GRPO's per-question normalization, which would otherwise cancel it. This is a per-question identity for a fixed reference: it fails when the reference includes the agent's own answer, which is how rewards for majority agreement collapse \citep{shafayat2025selftrain}. Leaving out the agent's group removes direct self-influence under the reliability model of Task~1.1, and we freeze references during each update phase and monitor accuracy on held-out questions with known answers. For score bands, exact agreement gives way to the correlated-agreement score \citep{shnayder2016informed}.

\slab{Debate messages.} Each message $m_t$ is credited by how far it moves the judge toward the decision:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{credit}_t=\omega_q\,\big[\,P_J(D_q\mid m_{1:t})-P_J(D_q\mid m_{1:t-1})\,\big],
\label{eq:credit}
\end{equation}
where $P_J$ is the judge's probability of the decision $D_q$ given the debate so far, obtained by re-scoring each prefix of the logged transcript, and $\hat\eta_q$ here comes from that probability, calibrated on development questions. The credits of a debate sum to $\omega_q$ times the change in the judge's probability of the decision, so (\ref{eq:credit}) splits that change among messages, telescoping as potential-based shaping does \citep{ng1999policy} (its potential depends on the decision, so policy invariance does not carry over), and credits each message with its effect given the earlier ones \citep{wolpert2001optimal,agogino2008analyzing}. 

\slab{Which debates count.} Because a persuasive message can move the judge toward a wrong decision, \textbf{messages are credited only in debates whose disputed step the judge settled with reproduced tool evidence}. Such a decision is a high-confidence label, not an answer key: the tool's data can be out of date, and re-running settles only the disputed step, not the steps both sides accepted. But it rests on evidence rather than persuasion, so, while such decisions are right more often than wrong, a message's expected credit is a positive multiple of how far it moved the judge toward the true answer. A decision settled by argument alone is not used as a label: after such a debate, only first answers are trained, against the rest of the group. We test this rule against rewards for debate wins and for the final decision at matched compute; at scale, a multi-agent process reward model trained on logged debates, extending our FG-PRM, will predict each message's credit. 

\slab{Safeguards.} Decisions involving flagged agents are dropped, planted cases never enter training, agents are rewarded separately, agents that judge each other never share a reward, and no judge is trained on its own rulings \citep{shi2026collusion}. Training uses policy-gradient methods \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}.

\hd{Preliminary results.} As a verifier, our FG-PRM, which labels each step with one of six hallucination types, substantially improves accuracy on GSM8K and MATH \citep{li2025fgprm}. Rewarding each search decision of our HiPRAG agents, not only the final answer, cut unnecessary searches from over 27\% to 2.3\% while also reducing missed ones \citep{wu2026hiprag}, so credit for single steps, which (\ref{eq:credit}) gives to debate messages, can change what agents do. \pending{In a pilot training a 1.5B-parameter model on GSM8K with GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}, with three of eight reference agents sharing a flawed hint, rewarding majority agreement raised held-out accuracy from 0.62 to 0.68 and then collapsed it to 0.41 within 300 steps, while reward (\ref{eq:firstanswer}) raised it to 0.73 without collapse: 73\% of the gain from training on true answers (to 0.77).}
% If this pilot is not run before submission, replace the \pending sentence with this expected outcome:
% A pilot will train a 1.5B-parameter model on GSM8K with GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}, with three of eight reference agents sharing a flawed hint: we expect rewards for agreeing with the model's own majority answers to rise and then collapse \citep{shafayat2025selftrain}, and reward (\ref{eq:firstanswer}) to rise without collapse while its reference is right more often than wrong.

\hd{Evaluation and risks.} On all three testbeds, we compare our rewards with rewards for majority agreement and for debate wins \citep{arnesen2024selfplay} at matched compute, on open-weight models from several families, measuring the accuracy of decisions and first answers on held-out questions, topics, and model families, including where human judges are unreliable \citep{burns2024weak}, and dishonest behavior during training: invented evidence, unsupported changes of answer, and wrong decisions the red team gets through. H5 predicts that our rewards gain more than rewards for agreement or debate wins, recover at least half the gain from training on known answers, and do not raise dishonest behavior.

\slab{Checking the premise.} The testbeds' known answers, which Task~3's rewards never use, also test its premise: we measure how often the labels are wrong, by topic and kind of reward, which checks $\eta_q<\frac12$ directly, and train the same agents on the known answers (standard RLVR) as an upper bound, reporting the share of its gain each label-free reward recovers. A misconception most agents share makes $\eta_q$ exceed one half on some questions, and peer prediction can then reward it \citep{denisovblanch2026consensus}. Task~2.2's random human audits also estimate each topic's error rate at no added cost, and training on a topic stops once an anytime-valid test puts that rate above a preset threshold below one half; on the testbeds, held-out accuracy on known answers also detects this, and all training stops if it falls. Training on the same decisions can make agents alike, so we measure shared error factors before and after training and train agents on different subsets of decisions.

\hd{Results and new knowledge.} If successful, the project will produce:

\section{Data Management Plan}\label{sec:dmp}
\small
\hd{Types of data, software, and other materials.} The project will produce (1)~three testbeds of questions with known answers: math and code problems with planted shared mistakes (A), multi-document questions composed from event structures, including synthetic maritime scenarios (B), and scientific-claim and peer-review questions with planted errors (C); (2)~logs of agent answers, ratings, debates, tool calls and their outputs, judge rulings, and audit outcomes; (3)~software: the agentic debate harness, the shared-error-factor reliability model, the impartial selection mechanism, the audit scheme, red-team agents, reinforcement-learning code, and the simulation code behind the theoretical results; and (4)~fine-tuned weights of open-weight models, where the original licenses permit release.

\hd{How the data will be acquired.} Testbed A draws on public benchmarks (GSM8K and MATH, released under the MIT license) and public programming problems. Testbed B is generated by our MEQA pipeline from event structures; its maritime scenarios are fully synthetic and unclassified. Testbed C uses public papers and the public reviews and rebuttals posted on OpenReview, under the platform's terms, together with published reproducibility reports. Logs are produced by running agents built on open-weight models on UT Dallas computers and on commercial models through their interfaces. No human-subjects data are collected; in experiments, human audits are simulated by revealing known answers.

\hd{Time and location of acquisition.} Not scientifically pertinent: all data are generated or collected computationally at UT Dallas during the 36-month project.

\hd{Processing.} Questions are normalized to a common schema, deduplicated, and split into development, evaluation, and held-out sets by topic and by model family. Planted cases are generated by versioned scripts that record each edit and its effect on the answer. Logs are stored as produced and then summarized for analysis; every number in a publication is regenerated by a released script from the stored logs.

\hd{File formats and naming conventions.} Questions and logs are stored as JSON Lines and Parquet; code is Python, released under the Mozilla Public License 2.0, as in the PI's earlier projects; model weights use the safetensors format. File names give the testbed, split, and version (for example, \texttt{tbB\_eval\_v1.2.jsonl}), with semantic versioning and a change log for each release.

\hd{Quality assurance and quality control.} The known answer of every generated question is confirmed by construction and by an independent script; two students review a random sample of each release, and disagreements are resolved before release. Each log record carries the model version, prompt version, random seed, and a hash of tool outputs, so that any debate can be re-run. The harness is frozen at each milestone, and automated runs confirm that frozen versions reproduce stored results.

\hd{Origin of existing data.} GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, MATH \citep{hendrycks2021math}, the MEQA benchmark and pipeline \citep{li2024meqa}, public OpenReview reviews, published reproducibility reports, and PeerEval logs produced by the PI's group. Each release records the source and license of every component.

\hd{Standards for data and metadata.} Datasets will carry Croissant machine-learning metadata and a datasheet describing their purpose, composition, collection, and known limitations; code repositories will include environment files that pin software versions.

\hd{Preservation and access.} Code will be public on GitHub, with each milestone release archived on Zenodo under a DOI. Testbeds and logs will be released on Hugging Face and on project webpages linked from the PI's lab website, by the time of the associated publication, and kept available for at least five years after the project ends. Working copies stay on access-controlled lab machines, with secondary copies on departmental systems at least once per semester; HPC scratch storage is never used as an archive. Final reports will be submitted as required by DoD Instruction 3200.12.

\hd{Balance of value and cost.} We will keep all data behind published results. Raw logs of exploratory runs, which can be large, will be kept for one year and then reduced to summaries, since the released code can regenerate them.

\hd{Security.} All data are public or synthetic, unclassified, and contain no controlled unclassified information or personally identifiable information. If any data were later found to raise national security concerns, they would not be released, in accordance with DoD Instruction 5230.09.
