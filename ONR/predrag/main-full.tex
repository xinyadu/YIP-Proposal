\section{Overview and Objectives}\label{sec:overview}

\subsection{The Problem and Why It Matters}\label{sec:problem}
AI agents built on large language models (LLMs) increasingly evaluate one another and decide together: they judge one another's answers, peer-review papers, supply the rewards that train new models, and vote or discuss to reach a group answer \citep{zheng2023judging,luo2025llm4sr,lee2024rlaif,du2024debate}. Yet a single LLM judge has known biases, such as favoring longer answers, the answer it reads first, or answers like its own \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024fair,panickssery2024self}. Collective reasoning can do better than a single judge \citep{verga2024juries,du2024debate}, but averaging or voting stays biased when the judges share mistakes, and open discussion lets a persuasive agent carry the group \citep{amayuelas2024attack}. \textbf{The central problem of this project is how a group of AI agents of unknown and correlated reliability, some possibly dishonest, can reach trustworthy decisions and evaluations.}

For example, five AI agents read shipping reports and are asked whether a cargo vessel has left port. Three say yes and two say no, but the vessel is still in port. Two of the yes answers rest on the same outdated schedule, and the third comes from a compromised agent that argues confidently for yes. A majority vote picks the wrong answer, and open discussion may do no better, because the compromised agent is persuasive and the two agents with the same source back each other up. We return to this example throughout (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

Verifying each claim directly, by an analyst or a satellite image, is slow and costly, and AI agents produce claims faster than people can verify them. We propose debate as a foundation for trustworthy evaluation by AI: two agents argue for opposite answers, exposing each other's flaws, until the dispute narrows to one step a judge can verify \citep{irving2018debate}. To work among many AI agents, debate must overcome three failures in the example, and a fourth arises once the agents learn from the group:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(F\arabic*),leftmargin=2.4em]
\item \textbf{Shared mistakes.} Agents that share a mistake seem to confirm each other, so a vote counts one error several times.
\item \textbf{Dishonest agents.} A dishonest agent can argue, rate others, or judge strategically, and several dishonest agents can collude.
\item \textbf{A judge that sees only words.} A judge that reads only text may favor the more persuasive side, even when one database query would settle the step in dispute.
\item \textbf{Rewards that track agreement.} Agents trained to match the group's answer are rewarded for conforming and can learn to agree on mistakes; in the example, a majority vote would reward the three agents that said yes.
\end{enumerate}

\label{sec:matters}All four failures have been measured. Model errors are correlated, most of all among larger, more accurate models \citep{kim2025correlated,goel2025great}, and nine AI judges from seven model families were worth about two independent ones \citep{kohli2026nine}. Pairs of agents verifying each other's work colluded in 94\% of runs when cheating paid more \citep{shi2026collusion}. One persuasive agent can sharply cut a group's accuracy \citep{amayuelas2024attack}; one round of discussion made AI judges nearly unanimous without making them more accurate \citep{shu2026unanimity}; and training a model on its own majority answers ended in a sudden collapse of accuracy \citep{shafayat2025selftrain}.

These failures spread: AI judges supply the rewards that train models \citep{bai2022constitutional,lee2024rlaif}, so their mistakes are copied into the models they train, and AAAI-26 gave an AI-generated review to each of the 22,977 main-track papers that entered full review \citep{biswas2026aaai}. As AI systems surpass people at more tasks, people cannot easily evaluate the systems' answers or their debates, so oversight will rely on AI evaluating AI, with people auditing a small part of the work \citep{bowman2022measuring,burns2024weak}.

Navy decisions face all of these problems at once: analysts fuse many reports with several AI tools of unknown reliability, many reports copy one earlier report, the true answer often arrives late, an adversary may forge a report or compromise an agent, and verification is costly when connectivity is limited (Section~\ref{sec:naval}).

\subsection{Objectives and Research Goals}\label{sec:goals}
We propose a framework in which groups of AI agents reach trustworthy decisions and evaluations through debate (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). Each agent first answers alone, with its evidence. Our central hypothesis is that a group of AI agents can decide more reliably than a single judge or a vote when it (i)~weighs each judgment by a credibility estimated without an answer key, counting shared mistakes once; (ii)~settles conflicts with evidence that an impartial judge can reproduce; and (iii)~learns from its own decisions through rewards that agreement alone cannot raise. The project has three interconnected research goals (RGs); each task rests on one principle whose properties we prove, and each property controls one source of error (Section~\ref{sec:framework}, Table~\ref{tab:principles}).

\textbf{RG1: Decide what to debate, and pick debaters and judges impartially} (Section~\ref{sec:rg1}). Task~1.1 weighs answers so that a group sharing a mistake counts at most once (F1) and sends a question to debate only when that is worth its cost; Task~1.2 picks debaters and a judge so that no agent's ratings on a question can change its own chance of a role on it (F2).

\textbf{RG2: Settle disputes by agentic debate, and catch collusion} (Section~\ref{sec:rg2}). Task~2.1 replaces open discussion with debate in an agentic harness, in which each side disputes a step of the other's argument and an impartial judge, whose choice is the group's decision, re-runs the tool evidence (F3); Task~2.2 catches colluders with planted cases and random human audits (F2).

\textbf{RG3: Let agents self-improve with reinforcement learning on the group's decisions} (Section~\ref{sec:rg3}). Task~3 scores each first answer against the answer the rest of the group favors without that agent's group, beyond chance agreement, and credits each debate message by how far it moved the judge toward the decision (F4). When that reference is more often right than wrong, training follows the agents' true accuracy in expectation, without human labels, so it can keep working after the agents outgrow human judges.

Three testbeds (math and code, multi-document questions with maritime scenarios, and scientific claims and peer review) vary how widely mistakes are shared and how many agents are dishonest, and a red team trains agents to collude \citep{greenblatt2024control} (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\subsection{Intellectual Merit: What Is New, and Why It Will Succeed}\label{sec:merit}
\hd{What is new.} (i)~A rater model for agents that answer and argue, in which a group sharing a mistake counts at most once and the posterior decides which questions need a debate, even unanimous ones; and an impartial way to choose debaters and judges. (ii)~A debate harness in which each side disputes a step of the other's argument and an impartial judge re-runs its evidence. (iii)~Audits on planted cases and random real decisions that expose colluding debaters and judges, even those that behave well only on planted cases. (iv)~Self-improvement from group decisions, with first-answer rewards that conformity cannot raise and message credit set by an impartial judge that re-runs evidence.

\hd{Why it will succeed.} Each task extends an established principle to agents that share mistakes, argue, and may collude (Table~\ref{tab:principles}). Most tasks build on the PI's previous work: PRD and the PeerEval leaderboard \citep{li2024prd}, FG-PRM \citep{li2025fgprm}, HiPRAG \citep{wu2026hiprag}, and MEQA \citep{li2024meqa} (Section~\ref{sec:pi}).

\subsection{Future Naval Relevance}\label{sec:naval}
\hd{ONR S\&T Focus Area.} Artificial Intelligence and Autonomy (Intelligent and Collaborative Agents; Adversarial AI \& Autonomy). The 2026 ONR S\&T Strategy will explore validating AI-produced results by ``human review, experimental confirmation or independent AI critique,'' using AI to ``validate, red-team and quality-check'' results under human oversight \citep{onr2026strategy}; this project supplies methods for such critique when the critics themselves may err together or collude. It also answers the Mathematical Data Science program's call for ``rigorous mathematical and algorithmic answers to questions that are currently addressed using heuristics or non-principled approaches'' \citep{onr2026mds}.

\hd{Navy needs.} The problem arises wherever several AI tools report on one question and some reports are copies, stale, forged, or produced by a compromised tool:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Maritime domain awareness}: position broadcasts can be spoofed \citep{marad2024ais}, and most industrial fishing vessels are not publicly tracked \citep{paolo2024satellite}, so decisions must combine port records, patrol reports, and imagery.
\item \emph{Intelligence analysis}: analysts must describe the ``quality and credibility of underlying sources'' \citep{odni2015icd203}, and repeated reports from one source can give ``an impression of confirming evidence'' \citep{wmd2005report}.
\item \emph{Decision support by networks of agents}: the Department of War is developing AI agents ``for AI-enabled battle management and decision support'' \citep{dow2026aistrategy}, and Marine Corps decision aids seek trust ``properly aligned to accuracy of results'' \citep{onr2026tacticalai}.
\item \emph{Evaluating AI before fielding}: more data and expertise are needed ``to quantify performance and risk of AI-enabled systems'' \citep{dote2026annual}, as AI judges increasingly score other AI systems.
\item \emph{Maintenance, logistics, cyber defense, and collaborative autonomy}: conflicting logs and records, alerts triaged by several tools, and unmanned systems that share reports under limited communications.
\end{itemize}
Across these uses, Task~1.1 counts copied and stale reports once; Tasks~1.2, 2.1, and~2.2 keep a compromised agent from rating its way into a role, discard evidence that does not reproduce, and flag colluders; Task~3 improves agents when ground truth arrives late; and each decision comes with its reasons.

\hd{Path to transition.} The project is fundamental research. Its products are methods with stated guarantees, an open harness, testbeds, and protocols that Navy laboratories, such as NRL's Navy Center for Applied Research in Artificial Intelligence, could evaluate on Navy data. The PI will pursue ONR Summer Faculty Research Program and Naval Research Enterprise Internship Program placements for students to work with Navy scientists on such evaluations.

\section{How the Problem Is Solved Today, and the Limitations}\label{sec:related}
Six lines of work bear on the problem. We build on each and note the gap that remains (Table~\ref{tab:compare}).

\hd{AI judges and voting.} LLM judges are standard for evaluating models and training them with AI feedback \citep{zheng2023judging,bai2022constitutional,lee2024rlaif}. Panels of judges \citep{verga2024juries} and majority votes over sampled answers \citep{wang2023selfconsistency} reduce single-judge biases, but they count every vote equally, so correlated judges add little: when two models are both wrong, they give the same wrong answer about 60\% of the time on one leaderboard \citep{kim2025correlated}.

\hd{Estimating reliability without an answer key.} For two answers and independent raters of known accuracy, the error-minimizing rule weights each vote by the rater's log-odds of being right \citep{nitzan1982optimal,shapley1984optimizing}. \citet{dawid1979maximum} estimate the accuracies by expectation--maximization (EM), and later work adds item difficulty \citep{whitehill2009whose}, provably good starting points \citep{zhang2016spectral}, dependence between classifiers \citep{jaffe2016dependent}, and detection of copying sources \citep{dong2009dependence}. Recent work models dependence among LLM judges with a shared latent factor \citep{balasubramanian2026ising} or shared confounders \citep{zhao2026care}. These methods treat raters as passive, never arguing or gaming the estimate, and do not say which questions need more than a vote.

\hd{Computational social choice.} Epistemic social choice asks which voting rules recover a true answer from independent noisy votes \citep{caragiannis2016noisy}. Impartial selection ensures that a participant's ratings cannot change its own chance of selection \citep{holzman2013impartial}, by having groups rate only outsiders \citep{alon2011sum,aziz2016strategyproof} or by randomizing among candidates whose ratings could change the outcome \citep{kurokawa2015impartial,kahng2018wily}; fair lotteries select panels \citep{flanigan2021fair}, and peer prediction scores agreement with peers beyond chance \citep{dasgupta2013crowdsourced,shnayder2016informed}. \citet{lev2023peernomination} add reliability weights, but they reward agreeing with the majority, which fails once accurate reviewers are a minority. In our setting, some claims can be verified, impartial mechanisms only choose who argues and who judges while debate decides the answer, and participants can persuade and collude while the decision is made.

\hd{Discussion and debate among LLMs.} Most multi-agent LLM systems use open discussion, revising until agents agree \citep{du2024debate,chan2024chateval,li2024prd}. Prior work on LLM discussion is limited: majority voting explains most of its gain \citep{choi2025debate}, one round can make judges nearly unanimous without making them more accurate \citep{shu2026unanimity}, and it assumes honest agents that exchange only text. Standard debate \citep{irving2018debate} assigns two debaters opposite answers and helps judges who know less than the debaters \citep{michael2023debate,kenton2024scalable}: with quotations verified against the source, non-expert AI judges reached 76\% accuracy and human judges 88\%, against 48\% and 60\% without debate \citep{khan2024debating}. But it trusts the judge, assumes the sides truly compete, and grounds claims at most in quoted text. Recent systems discuss only when agents are unsure \citep{eo2025down}, though in one study most items where discussion helped were unanimous but wrong \citep{hu2026scouting}, or give agents tools \citep{jeong2026toolmad} or a tool-using verifier for deadlocks \citep{li2026dynadebate}, but no judge re-runs the agents' own calls; judges shown a log claiming that tool calls succeeded accepted most failed outputs \citep{xu2026log}. We know of no debate protocol designed for many agents that share mistakes or collude.

\hd{AI control and adversarial crowdsourcing.} AI control keeps protocols safe even if a model subverts them, using a small budget of trusted audits and monitors validated on planted examples \citep{greenblatt2024control}, and in adversarial crowdsourcing a number of trusted ratings that does not grow with the number of items suffices even when honest raters are a minority \citep{steinhardt2016avoiding}. But colluding workers learn to recognize gold questions \citep{checco2018gold}, and capable LLMs can often tell an evaluation from real use \citep{needham2025evaluated}, which motivates our two kinds of audits.

\hd{Self-improvement without labels.} Models improve without labels by training on answers most samples agree on \citep{huang2023selfimprove,zuo2025ttrl}, on multi-agent discussion \citep{subramaniam2025multiagent,park2025maporl}, or on debate self-play \citep{arnesen2024selfplay}, but the reward is the weak point. Rewarding majority agreement collapsed accuracy \citep{shafayat2025selftrain}, and label-free intrinsic rewards tend to rise and then collapse \citep{he2026urlvr}; rewarding debate wins held off reward hacking, but with a weaker judge the critic learned to fabricate quotes and flaws \citep{kenton2026debate}; and rewarding approval taught models to mislead evaluators \citep{wen2025mislead}. Peer prediction trains LLMs toward truthfulness without labels, but its guarantees exclude collusion \citep{qiu2026peer}, and rewarding agreement with other models' majority does not correct for chance agreement and relies on model diversity against shared mistakes \citep{yang2026corl}. Process rewards score one agent's steps \citep{lightman2024verify,li2025fgprm}, and debate self-play credits each speech toward its own side \citep{arnesen2024selfplay}, not toward the group's decision.

\section{Technical Approach}\label{sec:approach}

\subsection{Problem Setting and Formal Framework}\label{sec:setting}
\hd{Agents and questions.} A pool of AI agents, each built on an underlying model (which several may share) with tools and role instructions, handles a stream of questions. A question $q$ asks for a decision or an evaluation with a small set of candidate answers $\mathcal{Y}$, such as yes or no, a choice among options, or a score band for a paper. For each question, the harness assigns at random which agents answer and which are held out as possible judges, so no agent can become a judge by declining to answer. Each answering agent first answers alone, giving an answer $x_{aq}$, a step-by-step argument, and its evidence.

\hd{Unknowns, resources, and threat model.} The true answer $y_q$, each agent's skill, which agents share mistakes, and which are dishonest are unknown. Available are planted cases (questions whose answers we know, mixed into the stream), a small, fixed budget of human audits (a person or a direct measurement finds the right answer for decisions drawn at random), and tools whose results can be re-run in a sandbox. Dishonest agents may coordinate and may answer, rate, argue, or judge strategically; they know the protocol but not its random draws, and may try to recognize planted cases. Honest agents may still share mistakes, for example from similar training data.

\hd{Output and design rules.} Each decision comes with its reasons: whose answers carried weight, which step decided the debate, and which evidence the judge reproduced. Five rules hold throughout. (R1)~Every answering agent answers alone, before seeing the others. (R2)~Peer ratings pick only who argues; the weighted vote (for a clear leader) or the judge (after a debate) picks the answer. (R3)~Tool evidence counts only if the judge re-runs it and obtains the same result. (R4)~Audits only catch collusion; they never settle a debate, and nothing triggers a human audit, so its cost stays fixed. (R5)~Planted cases never enter training, so agents cannot learn to spot them.

\hd{Formal framework: where a group's errors come from.}\label{sec:framework} Each task controls one source of error in the group's decision. Let $\pi_q$ be the posterior probability of the leading answer $\hat y_q$ from Task~1.1, and let the question go to debate when $\pi_q<\tau$. The decision $D_q$ is $\hat y_q$ without a debate and the judge's choice after one, and its chance of being wrong splits into terms that the tasks control:
\begin{equation}
P(D_q\neq y_q)\;\le\;\underbrace{(1-\tau)\,P(\pi_q\ge\tau)}_{\text{no debate: Task 1.1}}\;+\;P(\pi_q<\tau)\,\big[\underbrace{\epsilon_{\text{cand}}}_{\text{Task 1.1}}+\underbrace{\epsilon_{\text{role}}}_{\text{Tasks 1.2, 2.2}}+\underbrace{\epsilon_{\text{step}}}_{\text{Task 2.1}}\big].
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
The first term holds when the posterior is calibrated (answers given probability $\pi$ are right a fraction $\pi$ of the time); a vote that ignores shared mistakes is overconfident and breaks it (Figure~\ref{fig:rg1}(a)), so Task~1.1 supplies a posterior that accounts for them and sets $\tau$ by weighing a debate's cost against the stakes. In a debate, $\epsilon_{\text{cand}}$ is the chance that neither debated answer is right, $\epsilon_{\text{role}}$ the chance that a dishonest agent holds a role and steers the outcome, and $\epsilon_{\text{step}}$ the chance that a debate without such steering still ends wrong. Task~1.2 keeps any agent's ratings on a question from raising its own chance of a role on it and Task~2.2 flags colluders, so $\epsilon_{\text{role}}$ shrinks as audits accumulate; Task~2.1 reduces the judge's task to single disputed steps whose evidence it re-runs, so wherever a tool applies, the judge's share of $\epsilon_{\text{step}}$ approaches the tools' error rate; and Task~3 lowers the agents' own error rates, which can shrink every term. Removing a task leaves its term uncontrolled, and our ablations measure each term (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\subsubsection{Task 1.1: Deciding what to debate, counting shared mistakes once}\label{sec:t11}
\hd{Challenge.} A vote is informative only to the extent that voters err independently, and correlated votes can void the jury theorem's guarantee \citep{ladha1992condorcet}. In Figure~\ref{fig:rg1}(a), five independent agents, each right 70\% of the time, reach 84\% by majority vote, but agents copying one source of the same accuracy drag the vote to 70\%, and a rater model that assumes independence falls sooner, reading the copies' agreement as skill. LLM errors are correlated in practice \citep{kim2025correlated,goel2025great,kohli2026nine}.

\hd{Model and method.} Each answer counts according to its agent's track record, estimated from who agrees with whom across real questions, as one grades an exam without the answer key. We extend the rater model of \citet{dawid1979maximum} with shared error factors; unlike recent dependence-aware aggregators for LLM judges \citep{balasubramanian2026ising,zhao2026care}, a group shares a wrong answer, not a label-independent tilt or style effect, its weight is bounded (Proposition~1), and the posterior decides what to debate. Agents are partitioned into groups $g$, most with one member. On each question, a group has a hidden shared answer $s$, such as what an outdated schedule says: the true answer with probability $1-\lambda_g$, and a wrong answer $w$ with probability $\lambda_g\nu_g(w\mid y_q)$. Each member $a$ reports $s$ with its own errors, given by its confusion matrix $\theta_a$ (the chance of each answer it gives for each value of $s$). With $\kappa_g(s\mid y)=1-\lambda_g$ if $s=y$ and $\lambda_g\nu_g(s\mid y)$ otherwise,
\begin{equation}
P(x_{\cdot q}\mid y_q)=\prod_{g}G_{gq}(y_q),\qquad G_{gq}(y)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{Y}}\kappa_g(s\mid y)\prod_{a\in g_q}\theta_a(s,x_{aq}),
\label{eq:sharedfactor}
\end{equation}
where $g_q$ is the set of members of $g$ that answered $q$; for one-member groups $\lambda_g=0$, which is the original model. Decisions use Bayes' rule, the posterior over $y_q$ given all answers; with independent agents, two answers, and equal prior odds, this is the classical error-minimizing vote, in which an agent right with probability $p_a$ has weight $\log\frac{p_a}{1-p_a}$ \citep{nitzan1982optimal,shapley1984optimizing}. Parameters are fitted by EM over all real questions, with audit flags as a prior that part of an agent's answers are adversarial; candidate partitions come from clustering agents by agreement, each fitted from a vote in which each candidate group counts once, and the one with the best penalized likelihood is kept. Shared sources serve only as hints, since agents can read the same report and reason differently. In the vessel example, the two agents with the outdated schedule have often given the same unusual answers (Figure~\ref{fig:rg1}(b)), so together they count about once, and with no clear leader the question goes to debate.

\hd{Why this method.} \textit{Proposition~1 (a group counts at most once).} For yes-or-no questions, a group with $\lambda_g\le\frac12$ changes the log-odds of the answer by at most $\log\frac{1-\lambda_g}{\lambda_g}$, whatever its size and whatever its members answer. \textit{Proof.} Let $r$ be the ratio of the probabilities of the members' answers when $s$ is yes and when it is no. By (\ref{eq:sharedfactor}), the group's likelihood ratio is $\big[(1-\lambda_g)r+\lambda_g\big]/\big[(1-\lambda_g)+\lambda_g r\big]$, which increases with $r$ and lies between $\frac{\lambda_g}{1-\lambda_g}$ and $\frac{1-\lambda_g}{\lambda_g}$.\hfill$\square$

So a group carries at most the weight of one agent that errs with probability $\lambda_g$, whereas under independence each member would multiply the ratio again; with more answers and wrong answers equally likely, the bound becomes $\log\big[(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)\frac{1-\lambda_g}{\lambda_g}\big]$.

\textit{Proposition~2 (learning without an answer key).} Fix the partition, and suppose every answer has positive prior probability, at least three groups answer each question, their answers are independent given the true answer, and each group's answer distributions under different true answers are linearly independent (as when its members' confusion matrices are invertible and $\lambda_g<\frac12$). Then each group's distribution of answers given the true answer, which is all the posterior uses, is identifiable from the answers alone, up to relabeling, by Kruskal's theorem on three-way arrays \citep{kruskal1977three,allman2009identifiability}. With only two such groups it generally is not: different track records explain the same answers equally well, so no method that sees only answers can tell an accurate majority from one that copies a mistake.

Proposition~2 states the method's limit in advance, and our experiments include pools where it binds; Figure~\ref{fig:rg1}(a) shows that the model can be learned in practice.

\hd{Deciding what to debate.} With a calibrated posterior, letting the leading answer stand risks a loss $L$ with probability $1-\pi_q$, while a debate costs $c$ and, for two answers, ends wrong with a fixed probability $\epsilon_D$. A debate is worth its cost exactly when $L(1-\pi_q)-L\epsilon_D>c$, that is, when
\begin{equation}
\pi_q<\tau=1-\epsilon_D-c/L,
\label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
so higher stakes lead to more debates. Filtering matters because debate is not free or reliably better: it uses three to five times the tokens of a single answer and can overturn correct answers \citep{fan2026imad}, and it often fails to beat voting or self-consistency \citep{smit2024mad,choi2025debate}. The debate is between the two answers with the highest posterior, so $\epsilon_{\text{cand}}$ is the chance that the right answer is neither. We estimate $\epsilon_D$ on development questions with known answers, separately for disputes a tool can settle. Because $\pi_q$ counts effective votes, a unanimous answer from agents that share a mistake can still fall below $\tau$ and go to debate; rules triggered by low confidence or disagreement \citep{eo2025down} miss such questions, which were most of those where discussion helped in one study \citep{hu2026scouting}. Weighting cannot restore the information a correlated panel lacks \citep{kohli2026nine}, so deciding what to debate is the posterior's main job.

\hd{Research directions.} (a)~\emph{Detecting groups.} A copying group is exposed only where it gives an answer others do not. We will characterize how many questions it takes to detect a group, as a function of its size, $\lambda_g$, and how rarely others give its wrong answers, and when the partition itself is identifiable. (b)~\emph{Topics, difficulty, and calibration.} Topic-specific confusion matrices and question difficulty \citep{whitehill2009whose} keep hard questions from being read as copying, and the posterior is recalibrated on development questions.

\hd{Our previous work.} PRD's Peer Rank, which weights each AI reviewer by how highly the others rate its answers, agreed with human preferences more often than the strongest single judge (67.3\% vs.\ 64.3\% for GPT-4) \citep{li2024prd}; since such weights come from peers' ratings, models that share mistakes can raise one another's standing, which track records prevent.

\hd{Evaluation and risks.} On testbed A, where some agents receive the same flawed hint, we measure recovery of the seeded groups, accuracy, calibration, and questions needed to detect a group; plain Dawid--Skene is an ablation. H1 predicts a gain over majority vote that grows with how widely mistakes are shared and disappears with fewer than three independent groups. If EM converges poorly, we will start it from a spectral method \citep{zhang2016spectral} or use a pairwise measure of agreement on mistakes \citep{goel2025great}.

\subsubsection{Task 1.2: Impartial choice of debaters and judges}\label{sec:t12}
\hd{Challenge.} A debate needs a judge and one representative per competing answer. If agents choose them by rating one another, an agent can rate rivals low to be picked, agents built on similar models favor one another's outputs \citep{panickssery2024self,goel2025great}, and a judge that shares a model with one side has a stake in the outcome.

\hd{Core method.} Representatives are chosen by peer ratings of the arguments behind first answers, with raters weighted by track record, building on PRD and PeerEval \citep{li2024prd}; we adapt the partition idea of impartial selection \citep{alon2011sum,aziz2016strategyproof}. Agents sharing an underlying model form a group, fixed before any rating; $g(a)$ is agent $a$'s group, $H_q$ the agents that did not answer $q$, and $E_y$ the groups with a member answering $y$. On question $q$: (1)~the judge $J\sim\mathrm{Unif}(H_q)$ is drawn first and rates no one; (2)~for each debated answer $y$, a group $K_y$ is drawn from $E_y\setminus\{g(J)\}$ with probabilities $\pi_y$ that do not depend on ratings, avoiding groups drawn for other answers where possible; (3)~its representative is
\begin{equation}
R_y=\mathop{\arg\max}_{a\in K_y:\,x_{aq}=y}\ \sum_{b\in O_y(a)} w_b\,r_{ba},
\label{eq:rep}
\end{equation}
where $r_{ba}$ is $b$'s rating of $a$'s argument, $w_b$ is $b$'s track-record weight (Task~1.1), and the raters $O_y(a)$ are the agents outside $g(a)$, other than $J$, that answered $y$ or did not answer. An answer with no eligible group, such as the alternative to a unanimous answer sent to debate, gets a random agent outside $g(J)$, as in standard debate. Ratings pick only who argues; the judge picks the answer (Figure~\ref{fig:rg1}(c)).

\hd{Why this method.} \textit{Proposition~3 (impartiality and its price).} (a)~For any group $g$ and any two rating profiles that differ only in ratings given by members of $g$, each member of $g$ has the same probability of being the judge or a representative. (b)~If ratings are honest and rank each group's members by argument quality $Q\ge0$, then $\mathbb{E}[Q(R_y)]\ge\pi^*_y\,Q(a^*_y)$, where $a^*_y$ is the best agent answering $y$ and $\pi^*_y$ the probability that its group is drawn. \textit{Proof.} (a)~$J$, the debated answers, and every $K_y$ are drawn independently of ratings. If $K_y\ne g$, no member of $g$ represents $y$; if $K_y=g$, (\ref{eq:rep}) uses no rating from $g$, and the weights do not depend on ratings. (b)~Whenever $g(a^*_y)$ is drawn, (\ref{eq:rep}) selects $a^*_y$.\hfill$\square$

A single agent is a one-member coalition, so (a) gives impartiality in the sense of \citet{kurokawa2015impartial}, extended to collusion within a group. With uniform draws, (b) gives $1/|E_y|$ unless the judge's group holds $a^*_y$; with two or three groups per answer, this beats the $1/(1+m)$ guaranteed by randomizing over would-be winners \citep{kurokawa2015impartial} when one agent is chosen and each rates $m$ others. No answering agent rates the other side and the judge rates no one, so neither can rate the other side's best arguer low. A judge lottery that caps any agent's chance at $\bar p$ lets $f$ colluders hold the seat with probability at most $f\bar p$, so judge chances are kept as equal as possible \citep{flanigan2021fair}. Colluders in other groups, or held out, can still shift ratings, so we measure coalition gains.

\hd{Research directions.} (a)~Agents that share error factors, or recognize one another's outputs \citep{panickssery2024self}, will share a group even when their model labels differ. (b)~Drawing groups in proportion to a score that ratings cannot change, such as the best track record in the group, keeps Proposition~3(a) and raises $\pi^*_y$ when that score tracks quality; we will measure the price of impartiality and bound what coalitions gain.

\hd{Our previous work.} In PRD, with GPT-4 as the only reviewer, GPT-4's Elo rating (a chess-style rating) was 46 points above the one derived from human preferences, while weighted peer ranking placed all five models in the human order \citep{li2024prd}.

\hd{Evaluation and risks.} We measure manipulability, the largest gain in selection probability a strategic rater or coalition achieves by misreporting (found by the red team), the price of impartiality, and the accuracy of the resulting debates (H2), on PeerEval and testbed C. With few underlying models there are few groups; we will then diversify agents by role instructions and tools, or draw judges from a held-out pool of models that never answer.

\subsection{RG2: Settle Disputes by Agentic Debate, and Catch Collusion}\label{sec:rg2}
Open discussion erases disagreement and rewards persuasion, and debaters can collude. RG2 settles each dispute by debate before an impartial judge and audits the participants.


\subsubsection{Task 2.1: Debate before an impartial judge}\label{sec:t21}
\hd{Challenge.} Today's multi-agent systems mostly discuss until they agree, which erases the disagreement that exposes errors \citep{shu2026unanimity,choi2025debate}. Standard debate keeps the disagreement, but it runs fixed rounds of text between given models and leaves the judge to weigh words alone, so a persuasive debater can win a step that one database query would settle.

\hd{Core method.} We run debate in a harness, the software that turns models into agents \citep{ning2026harness,yao2023react,yang2024sweagent}, with five parts (Figure~\ref{fig:rg2}(a)). \emph{Role instructions} tell each debater which answer to defend, to argue in numbered steps, and to attach evidence where a tool applies, and tell the judge to rule only on disputed steps. The \emph{control loop} sets who speaks and who judges (picked in Task~1.2) and when to stop: the representatives lay out their arguments in numbered steps, each names the first step of the other's argument that it disputes, a short exchange follows, and the loop stops for the judge, who upholds or strikes each disputed step \citep{irving2018debate}. \emph{Tools}, such as document search, databases, and code execution, are optional. \emph{Verification}: tool evidence carries its exact call (tool, arguments, data version), which the judge, itself an agent \citep{zhuge2025agent}, re-runs; evidence that does not reproduce is discarded. \emph{Memory} logs every message, call, and result. In the vessel example, A4 disputes A3's step ``so V left at 06:00'' with a 09:10 port report listing V at its berth, and the judge re-runs A4's query and picks no (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

\hd{Why this method.} \textit{Proposition~4 (judging a question reduces to judging single steps).} Suppose that (i)~every argument whose steps are all true reaches the right answer, (ii)~the side defending the right answer argues with true steps and can find a false step in the other side's argument, and (iii)~the judge rules correctly on any single step it is shown. Then, if a side loses exactly when one of its disputed steps is struck, the side defending the right answer wins. \textit{Proof.} The other side's argument reaches a wrong answer, so by (i) it contains a false step, which the right side disputes and the judge strikes; any step of the right side's argument that the other side disputes is true, and the judge upholds it.\hfill$\square$

So the judge errs on a debated question only when (ii) or (iii) fails on a disputed step, the core idea of debate \citep{irving2018debate,browncohen2024doubly}. Re-running tool evidence makes (iii) plausible: a step a tool can settle is ruled on about as accurately as the tool itself, so the judge's share of $\epsilon_{\text{step}}$ in (\ref{eq:decomp}) approaches the tool's error rate, while the judge examines a few steps instead of two whole arguments. The guarantee fails where its assumptions do: a flaw hidden among many steps that are each hard to verify, the obfuscated-argument problem \citep{browncohen2025prover}; steps no tool can settle; and true but selectively chosen facts \citep{hu2026lying}.

\hd{Research directions.} (a)~\emph{Finding the false step.} We will propose candidate false steps with models that judge whether one claim implies another and with FG-PRM's step-level error detection \citep{li2025fgprm}, and recurse into a complex disputed step's sub-steps, following prover--estimator debate \citep{browncohen2025prover}. (b)~\emph{A right of reply.} Before the judge rules, each side may add context to the other side's evidence, against selectively chosen facts, and quotations are verified against the source \citep{khan2024debating}. Ablations remove each harness part, remove tools or re-running, and replace debate with open discussion among the same agents.

\hd{Our previous work.} In PRD's peer discussion, agreement with human preferences rose from 72.9\% (GPT-4 alone) to 75.0\% when GPT-3.5 led the discussion, but fell to 71.4\% when GPT-4 led \citep{li2024prd}: one reviewer can carry a discussion. HiPRAG's search agents, which ground answers in retrieved evidence \citep{wu2026hiprag}, will be our first tool-using debaters.

\hd{Evaluation and risks.} We measure accuracy on disputed questions, correct minorities that prevail and correct majorities overturned, how often re-running evidence changes the outcome, the judge's error on each kind of step, and cost. H3 predicts that debate beats open discussion among the same agents, more so as the share of steps a tool can settle grows. If neither side finds a false step, the judge reads both arguments, as in standard debate, and a close ruling is reported as unsettled, with its probability and the step in dispute; tool evidence can be out of date, so the judge weighs its provenance, and human audits measure how often this happens.

\subsubsection{Task 2.2: Automatic and human audits against collusion}\label{sec:t22}
\hd{Challenge.} Collusion means debaters, or the judge, steering a debate to the wrong side: arguing badly on purpose \citep{vanderweij2025sandbagging}, misleading with true but selectively chosen facts \citep{hu2026lying}, or favoring one side, none of which re-running evidence catches. LLM agents collude when it pays \citep{shi2026collusion}, can hide signals in ordinary text \citep{mathew2025hidden}, and can often tell evaluations from real use \citep{needham2025evaluated}.

\hd{Core method.} We use two kinds of audits (Figure~\ref{fig:rg2}(b)). \emph{Automatic audits} mix planted debates on planted cases into the real stream, formatted like real questions; a planted case can be a paper with a deliberately inserted error, as in studies of human peer review \citep{schroter2008errors}. \emph{Human audits} cover a few real decisions drawn at random after they are made: a person or a direct measurement, such as a satellite image, finds the right answer. Blame is assigned by comparing each agent with the others it was paired with across many random pairings. An agent whose audited decisions go wrong more often than its track record predicts, or that does much worse on audited real decisions than on planted ones of matched difficulty, is flagged; flags lower its weight in Task~1.1 and bar it from arguing or judging. Audits never change a decision, and nothing triggers a human audit.

\hd{Why this method.} \textit{Proposition~5 (random audits cannot be dodged).} If each real decision is audited independently with probability $\rho$, drawn after the decision is made, then every decision a colluder steers is audited with probability $\rho$, whatever the colluder can infer about planted cases, and the chance that its first $n$ steered decisions all escape audit is $(1-\rho)^n$, however long the stream. \textit{Proof.} The draw is independent of everything the colluder saw when it acted.\hfill$\square$

Planted cases add cheap evidence as long as they cannot be told from real questions; human audits keep the guarantee when they can. Random audits deter agents that can hide misconduct only if the evidence survives and the timing is unpredictable \citep{gans2026randomized,allingham1972tax}; immutable logs and after-the-fact draws provide both. Flags are raised by an anytime-valid sequential rule \citep{ramdas2023game}, which keeps the chance that an agent erring no more than predicted is ever flagged below a chosen level $\alpha$ (split across agents) however long monitoring runs; the number of audits needed to flag a colluder then depends on $\alpha$ and on how much more often its decisions go wrong, not on the number of decisions, in line with \citet{steinhardt2016avoiding}.

\hd{Research directions.} (a)~\emph{Planted cases that look real}, made from real questions by controlled edits, such as changing a timestamp in a port report, with a discriminator that guides the generator until it can no longer tell planted from real \citep{checco2018gold}. (b)~\emph{How many audits are enough.} Treating auditing as an inspection game \citep{avenhaus2002inspection}, we expect that a colluder that tunes how often it cheats can cause, before it is flagged, wrong decisions, beyond the honest error rate, that grow at most like the square root of the number of decisions when honest agents also err, and stay bounded when they rarely do; we will prove such bounds and compare them with the red team's best attacks. A colluder that mimics a weak honest agent is never flagged, so its harm is bounded instead by the weight and roles a weak record earns (Task~1.2(b)). (c)~\emph{Signs of coordination}, such as rulings that favor one debater more than the evidence explains \citep{dong2009dependence}, with messages paraphrased against hidden signals \citep{roger2023preventing}.

\hd{Our previous work.} The PI co-advised UT Dallas's team, one of five university red teams in the Amazon Nova AI Challenge \citep{sahai2025nova}, which built COMET, a closed-loop red-teaming framework that elicited harmful outputs from guarded code models \citep{comet2025}.

\hd{Evaluation and risks.} Against the red team, we measure wrong decisions that get through at each audit budget, time to flag colluders, false flags, and whether a discriminator can tell planted cases from real ones (H4). A group that cheats only once is caught with probability at most $\rho$, and if planted cases stay recognizable, flags rest on the human audits alone, which slows flagging.

\subsection{RG3: Let Agents Self-Improve with Reinforcement Learning on the Group's Decisions}\label{sec:rg3}
Self-improvement needs rewards that track correct answers, not votes or debate wins.

\subsubsection{Task 3: Rewards for contributions to trustworthy decisions}\label{sec:t3}
\hd{Challenge.} The obvious rewards fail (Section~\ref{sec:related}): majority agreement rewards conformity and can collapse accuracy \citep{shafayat2025selftrain}, debate wins reward persuasion when the judge can be fooled \citep{kenton2026debate}, and approval rewards misleading the evaluator \citep{wen2025mislead}. A group's reward must also say whose messages helped, or training rewards agents that contributed nothing and misses the agent that found the decisive evidence.

\hd{Core method.} We train the agents with reinforcement learning on the group's decisions, which need no human labels and count shared mistakes once. \emph{First answers.} Agent $a$'s first answer to $q$ is scored against $D_q^{-a}$, the answer that the posterior of Task~1.1 favors when $a$'s group is left out, minus the agreement of its answer to another question $q'$ with that reference on a third, $q''$, both on its topic and drawn at random:
\begin{equation}
r_a(q)=\omega_q\mathbf{1}\{x_{aq}=D_q^{-a}\}-\omega_{q''}\mathbf{1}\{x_{aq'}=D_{q''}^{-a}\},
\label{eq:firstanswer}
\end{equation}
so an agent earns credit for agreeing with the rest of the group beyond chance, the correlated-agreement form of peer prediction \citep{dasgupta2013crowdsourced,shnayder2016informed}. Unlike pairwise peer prediction \citep{qiu2026peer} or agreement with other models' majority \citep{yang2026corl}, the reference counts shared mistakes once and discounts flagged agents. \emph{Debate messages.} Message $m_t$ in a debate on $q$ is credited by how far it moves the judge toward the decision $D_q$:
\begin{equation}
r_t=\omega_q\,\big[\,p_J(D_q\mid m_{1:t})-p_J(D_q\mid m_{1:t-1})\,\big],
\label{eq:credit}
\end{equation}
where $p_J$ is the judge's probability of the decision given the debate so far, obtained by re-scoring each prefix of the logged transcript. For two answers, $\omega_q=1-2\hat\eta_q$ (with more, $1-\hat\eta_q|\mathcal{Y}|/(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)$), where $\hat\eta_q$ is the estimated chance that the reference is wrong: from the left-out posterior for first answers, and from the judge's probability, calibrated on development questions, for messages. For score bands, exact agreement gives way to the correlated-agreement score \citep{shnayder2016informed}. Decisions involving flagged agents are dropped, planted cases never enter training, and training uses standard policy-gradient methods \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. In the vessel example (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}), A4's message citing the port report moves the judge most and earns the most credit, and A3's unsupported claims move the judge away from the decision and are penalized, whereas a majority-agreement reward would have rewarded the three yes answers.

\hd{Why this method.} \textit{Proposition~6 (decisions as noisy labels).} Consider a yes-or-no question, and suppose that, given the true answer, $D_q^{-a}$ is wrong with probability $\eta_q$ independently of agent $a$'s answer. Then $\mathbb{E}\,\mathbf{1}\{x=D_q^{-a}\}=\eta_q+(1-2\eta_q)\,\mathbf{1}\{x=y_q\}$, so in expectation, rewarding agreement with $D_q^{-a}$ gives $1-2\eta_q$ times the policy-gradient update that rewarding the true answer would give; the second term of (\ref{eq:firstanswer}) does not depend on $x_{aq}$ and leaves this update unchanged. If $a$'s answers ignore the question, the two terms of (\ref{eq:firstanswer}) have the same expectation over questions, so such answers earn nothing. \textit{Proof.} $P(D_q^{-a}=x)$ is $1-\eta_q$ if $x=y_q$ and $\eta_q$ otherwise, and terms that do not vary with the answer do not change the policy gradient. Answers that ignore the question, $x_{aq}$ and $x_{aq'}$, share one distribution and are independent of $(\omega_q,D_q^{-a})$ and $(\omega_{q''},D_{q''}^{-a})$, which also share one distribution.\hfill$\square$

Proposition~6 explains each design choice. Every reference more often right than wrong pushes the agent toward the right answer, and $\omega_q$ weights each question by that push (an inverse-noise weight, not the unbiased correction of \citealp{natarajan2013learning}): confident references count fully, near-ties little. Independence fails when the reference includes the agent's own answer, since the agent can then raise its reward by agreeing with itself and agents like it, which is how rewards for majority agreement collapse \citep{shafayat2025selftrain}; leaving out the agent's group restores it under the model of Task~1.1, and subtracting chance agreement removes the reward for answers that ignore the question, such as always saying yes, even when colluders coordinate on them. For debate messages, the credits of a debate sum to $\omega_q$ times the change in the judge's probability of the decision, so (\ref{eq:credit}) splits that change among messages, telescoping as potential-based shaping does \citep{ng1999policy} (its potential depends on the decision, so policy invariance does not carry over), and credits each message with its effect given the earlier ones, in the spirit of difference rewards \citep{wolpert2001optimal,agogino2008analyzing}. When the judge decides by re-running evidence, the decision does not depend on persuasion, so messages earn credit for moving the judge toward what the evidence shows.

\hd{Research directions.} (a)~\emph{Credit at scale.} We will train a multi-agent process reward model on logged debates to predict each message's credit, with labels such as ``introduced re-runnable evidence'' and ``persuaded without evidence,'' extending FG-PRM, and compare it with order-free credit such as Shapley values. (b)~\emph{What improves.} We will measure whether training improves first answers, debating, and judging on held-out questions, topics, and model families, including where human judges are unreliable \citep{burns2024weak}. Agents are rewarded separately, agents that judge each other never share a reward, and no judge is trained on its own rulings \citep{shi2026collusion}.

\hd{Our previous work.} FG-PRM labels each step of a math solution with one of six hallucination types and, as a verifier, substantially improves accuracy on GSM8K and MATH \citep{li2025fgprm}; HiPRAG rewards each search decision of an agent, not only its final answer \citep{wu2026hiprag}.

\hd{Evaluation and risks.} On all three testbeds, we compare our rewards with rewards for majority agreement and for debate wins \citep{arnesen2024selfplay} at matched compute, on open-weight models from several families, measuring the accuracy of decisions and first answers on held-out questions and dishonest behavior during training: invented evidence, unsupported changes of answer, and wrong decisions the red team gets through (H5). A misconception most agents share makes $\eta_q$ exceed one half on some questions, and peer prediction can pay for it \citep{denisovblanch2026consensus}; accuracy on held-out questions with known answers detects this, and training stops if it falls. Training on the same decisions can make agents alike, so we measure shared error factors before and after training and train agents on different subsets of decisions. Debaters could learn to join the judge's likely decision instead of defending their answer; if so, we will credit messages for moving the judge toward the debater's own answer, which makes (\ref{eq:credit}) a shaped debate-win reward.

\hd{Results and new knowledge.} If successful, the project will produce (i)~a rater model that counts a group sharing a mistake at most once, with conditions for learning it without an answer key and rates for detecting groups; (ii)~an impartial mechanism for choosing debaters and judges, with its measured price and bounds on coalition gains; (iii)~an open agentic debate harness that reduces judging a question to judging single steps, with the judge's error measured for each kind of step; (iv)~an audit scheme with bounds on the harm a colluder can do before it is flagged; and (v)~a self-improvement method whose expected update follows accuracy rather than agreement. \hd{Limitations.} Where no tool applies, the judge rules on argument alone. \subsection{Gold Standard Science}\label{sec:gss}
The project will follow the tenets of Executive Order 14303, as the PI's previous work does.

\hd{Reproducible and transparent.} The harness logs every debate with its exact tool calls, model versions, prompts, and seeds, and tool evidence is re-runnable by design. Code, testbeds, prompts, and protocols will be released with each paper; the PI's group studies reproducibility directly \citep{yan2025lmrbench}.

\hd{Communicative of error and uncertainty.} Every guarantee is stated with its assumptions, decisions carry calibrated probabilities, and the framework's accuracy is estimated from random human audits with valid confidence intervals \citep{angelopoulos2023ppi}.

\hd{Skeptical and falsifiable.} A red team attacks our own methods, ablations remove each component, and each task states a hypothesis that experiments can refute (H1--H5, Table~\ref{tab:principles}) and the regime in which its method should fail. Metrics and analysis plans will be fixed before the main experiments.

\hd{Collaborative, peer reviewed, and open to negative results.} The work joins statistics, social choice, AI safety, and natural language processing, with results for peer-reviewed venues in each; failures of debate or audits will be published as findings.

\hd{Without conflicts of interest.} The PI will disclose outside activities and support as UT Dallas policy requires, and evaluations use public or synthetic data with released code.

\section{Management Approach}\label{sec:mgmt}
This is a single-PI award with no subawardees, co-investigators, or senior personnel. The PI will direct all research and be responsible for schedules, reports, and releases. The budget supports two Ph.D.\ students for three years, one month of PI summer effort per year, travel to ONR Project and Program Review Meetings and to conferences, and computing.

\hd{Team and roles.} Student~1 leads the statistical and mechanism-design work and its proofs: Tasks~1.1 and~1.2, the audit analysis of Task~2.2, and testbeds A and C. Student~2 leads the agent and learning work: the harness of Task~2.1, Task~3, and testbed B, which reuses the MEQA pipeline. Both students run the red team, rotating roles so that no one attacks only their own method. Code, logs, and planted-case generators live in one shared repository, with the harness frozen at each milestone so that results stay comparable.

\hd{Reporting and dissemination.} The PI will submit annual and final reports, attend Project and Program Review Meetings, publish in peer-reviewed venues, and release code, testbeds, and protocols at M1, M6, and with each paper.

\hd{Relation to other support.} The PI's NSF CAREER award studies event knowledge from long documents, the subject of the MEQA pipeline that testbed B reuses; the Open Philanthropy grant supports control evaluations of AI debate; the Amazon Research Award concerns process-guided fine-tuning. This proposal funds aims those awards do not, and any overlap with a pending proposal would be removed with the program officers.

\section{Data Management Plan}\label{sec:dmp}
\small
\hd{Types of data, software, and other materials.} The project will produce (1)~three testbeds of questions with known answers: math and code problems with planted shared mistakes (A), multi-document questions composed from event structures, including synthetic maritime scenarios (B), and scientific-claim and peer-review questions with planted errors (C); (2)~logs of agent answers, ratings, debates, tool calls and their outputs, judge rulings, and audit outcomes; (3)~software: the agentic debate harness, the shared-error-factor rater model, the impartial selection mechanism, the audit scheme, red-team agents, reinforcement-learning code, and the simulation code behind the theoretical results; and (4)~fine-tuned weights of open-weight models, where the original licenses permit release.

\hd{How the data will be acquired.} Testbed A draws on public benchmarks (GSM8K and MATH, released under the MIT license) and public programming problems. Testbed B is generated by our MEQA pipeline from event structures; its maritime scenarios are fully synthetic and unclassified. Testbed C uses public papers and the public reviews and rebuttals posted on OpenReview, under the platform's terms, together with published reproducibility reports. Logs are produced by running agents built on open-weight models on UT Dallas computers and on commercial models through their interfaces. No human-subjects data are collected; in experiments, human audits are simulated by revealing known answers.

\hd{Time and location of acquisition.} Not scientifically pertinent: all data are generated or collected computationally at UT Dallas during the 36-month project.

\hd{Processing.} Questions are normalized to a common schema, deduplicated, and split into development, evaluation, and held-out sets by topic and by model family. Planted cases are generated by versioned scripts that record each edit and its effect on the answer. Logs are stored as produced and then summarized for analysis; every number in a publication is regenerated by a released script from the stored logs.

\hd{File formats and naming conventions.} Questions and logs are stored as JSON Lines and Parquet; code is Python, released under the Mozilla Public License 2.0, as in the PI's earlier projects; model weights use the safetensors format. File names give the testbed, split, and version (for example, \texttt{tbB\_eval\_v1.2.jsonl}), with semantic versioning and a change log for each release.

\hd{Quality assurance and quality control.} The known answer of every generated question is confirmed by construction and by an independent script; two students review a random sample of each release, and disagreements are resolved before release. Each log record carries the model version, prompt version, random seed, and a hash of tool outputs, so that any debate can be re-run. The harness is frozen at each milestone, and automated runs confirm that frozen versions reproduce stored results.

\hd{Origin of existing data.} GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, MATH \citep{hendrycks2021math}, the MEQA benchmark and pipeline \citep{li2024meqa}, public OpenReview reviews, published reproducibility reports, and PeerEval logs produced by the PI's group. Each release records the source and license of every component.

\hd{Standards for data and metadata.} Datasets will carry Croissant machine-learning metadata and a datasheet describing their purpose, composition, collection, and known limitations; code repositories will include environment files that pin software versions.

\hd{Preservation and access.} Code will be public on GitHub, with each milestone release archived on Zenodo under a DOI. Testbeds and logs will be released on Hugging Face and on project webpages linked from the PI's lab website, by the time of the associated publication, and kept available for at least five years after the project ends. Working copies stay on access-controlled lab machines, with secondary copies on departmental systems at least once per semester; HPC scratch storage is never used as an archive. Final reports will be submitted as required by DoD Instruction 3200.12.

\hd{Balance of value and cost.} We will keep all data behind published results. Raw logs of exploratory runs, which can be large, will be kept for one year and then reduced to summaries, since the released code can regenerate them.

\hd{Security.} All data are public or synthetic, unclassified, and contain no controlled unclassified information or personally identifiable information. If any data were later found to raise national security concerns, they would not be released, in accordance with DoD Instruction 5230.09.
