\section*{Research objective}
\textbf{We propose a peer-evaluation framework in which AI models review one another's answers and collectively assess their quality.} Reviewers can share biases: Arena found a 37-percentage-point gap between three same-provider judges' preferences for their family and human votes \citep{selfpreference}. We use mutual agreement to learn how much influence each reviewer should have within this framework. The central question is when peer evaluation produces reliable answer comparisons and model rankings despite shared biases.

\section*{Peer-evaluation framework and preliminary evidence}
Models contribute answers and peer reviews. Reviewers compare anonymous answer pairs, and their votes are combined into answer-quality decisions and model rankings. The panel starts with equal reviewer weights. We score each reviewer's agreement with the weighted votes of reviewers from other model families, smooth and normalize these scores, and use them to update reviewer influence. The collective evaluation is then recomputed. This repeats until weights stabilize or reach an iteration limit. Human votes are held out to test the quality of the resulting peer evaluations.

Our prior peer-evaluation work \citep{prd} and \href{https://peereval-ai.github.io/}{PeerEval leaderboard and tool} \citep{peereval} provide the infrastructure for the project. A pilot covers 1,179 human-labeled battles with four judges. On 794 crowd battles, agreement weighting matched 67.8\% of votes, versus 65.7\% for equal weighting. These exploratory results motivate testing when agreement is useful evidence and when it repeats a shared bias.

\section*{Research approach}
\hd{1. Learn reviewer influence within peer evaluation.}
Six to eight peer reviewers from different model families will compare anonymous answer pairs in both display orders, choosing A, B, tie, or both bad. We will align the orders and average each reviewer's votes. At iteration $t$, reviewer $i$ receives an agreement score $a_i^{(t)}$: its mean agreement with the weighted votes of reviewers \emph{outside its own model family}. This comparison excludes the reviewer's own vote and family.

\noindent Starting from equal weights, we update
\nopagebreak
\[
w_i^{(t+1)}=(1-\eta)w_i^{(t)}+
\eta\,\frac{\varepsilon+a_i^{(t)}}{\sum_j(\varepsilon+a_j^{(t)})},
\]
where $0<\eta\le1$ controls how quickly weights change and $\varepsilon>0$ keeps every reviewer eligible. We stop when weights stabilize or reach a fixed iteration limit. The final weighted reviews determine answer preferences and model rankings. No human labels enter these updates.

\hd{2. Establish when peer evaluation is reliable.}
Agreement can reward a shared mistake, so we will study what prevents a group of similar judges from reinforcing itself. We will compare ordinary agreement, agreement excluding a judge's own family, and a version that limits any one family's total influence. We will analyze convergence, sensitivity to initialization, and how many correlated judges it takes to change a decision. Peer-assessed answer-quality weights \citep{prd} and a fixed combination of answer quality and agreement will serve as comparisons. These tests separate the benefit of the update rule from simply adding more judges.

\hd{3. Transfer peer evaluations to new questions and models.}
We will construct weights on development prompts, freeze them for unseen prompts, and compare this with recomputing weights on new unlabeled prompts. Later time windows and new answer-model families will test transfer. We will bootstrap questions and remove one judge family at a time to measure how stable the resulting rankings are. Instability will flag a result for additional review; we will test whether this flag predicts disagreement with people. For the proposed method, human votes are used only for evaluation.

\section*{Evaluation, outputs, and feasibility}
We will use up to 10,000 public and, subject to agreement, recent Arena battles. Baselines include the strongest single judge, equal weighting, answer-quality weighting, static agreement weights, and weights fitted to limited human votes. We will measure preference accuracy, log loss with fixed probability smoothing, ranking agreement, stability, and cost, with paired bootstrap intervals that rerun the weighting loop. Length-matched tests, balanced longer/shorter-answer cases, and a length heuristic will check whether apparent gains come from verbosity. Repeated human votes and display-order metadata will help interpret disagreements. Shared biases may still limit gains; we will report those failure cases.

\hd{Project scope and requested support.}
Months 1--3 will establish data and baselines; months 4--8 will develop the peer-evaluation methods; months 9--12 will complete transfer tests and release a paper, code, and evaluation reports. The \$50,000 gift allocates \$36,000 to graduate research, including fringe and tuition; \$10,000 to APIs and computing; and \$4,000 to evaluation annotations. At maximum scale, 10,000 pairs $\times$ eight judges $\times$ two orders require 160,000 judging calls; pilot costs will set the affordable scale. Results will complement Arena's voting and AutoEval \citep{autoeval}.

\formq{3--5 sentence summary of proposed research}
We propose a peer-evaluation framework in which AI models review one another's answers and collectively assess their quality. Mutual agreement determines how much influence each reviewer receives, with weights updated from consistency with reviewers in other model families. The framework produces answer preferences and model rankings through our PeerEval platform (\url{https://peereval-ai.github.io/}). We will study when peer evaluation is reliable and test accuracy, bias, and transfer on Arena data with human votes held out for evaluation. Outputs will include a paper, an open-source peer-evaluation tool, and reproducible evaluation reports.

\formq{Additional dataset access and why it is needed}
We request up to 10,000 recent, de-identified text battles with prompts, paired responses, model identifiers, preference outcomes including ties and both-bad, coarse timestamps, and per-vote display order. Randomization metadata and repeated independent votes on the same response pairs in both orders, where available, would help separate order effects from preference ambiguity. Recent battles enable tests on new models and changing preferences. Public data support the core study; additional access would follow Arena's agreements.

\formq{Expected outputs (paper, dataset, code release, etc.)}
A paper on peer evaluation, its reliability, and failure cases; an open-source implementation in PeerEval; and reproducible evaluations of preference prediction, model rankings, transfer, stability, and cost. We will release permitted judgments and analysis code, with restricted data governed by the access agreement. We welcome a joint blog post or talk with Arena.

\formq{Brief budget explanation (3--5 sentences)}
Approximately \$36,000 will support graduate research, including applicable fringe and tuition. Model APIs and computing will receive \$10,000, with pilot costs determining the final panel and sample size. Independent evaluation annotations will receive \$4,000. Allocations will follow UTD requirements; no indirect costs are included unless required by institutional policy.

\formq{Why is this work relevant to Arena?}
Arena's self-preference findings show why peer evaluation needs to account for reviewer biases, while its human preferences provide a strong independent test of AI reviews. Our project will test when peer evaluation produces reliable answer comparisons and model rankings, using mutual agreement to learn reviewer influence. The results could complement AutoEval, support evaluation of newly released models, and identify unstable rankings that warrant further review.
